# The Science of Musical Beauty: Psychology, Acoustics and Neuroscience of Why Music Sounds Good

Format note: each cited finding gives the finding, the source, and a "-> Do:" production/composition takeaway for a composer-AI. Items marked [unverified this session] come from background knowledge and were not confirmed against a source during this research pass; treat them with caution or verify before relying on them.

## 1. Consonance and dissonance: why some intervals and chords sound "smooth" and others "tense"

### Takeaway
Consonance is best explained by harmonicity (how closely the combined partials resemble a single harmonic series / a voice) more than by roughness alone, and the *preference* for consonance is substantially learned through Western music exposure. Dissonance is a resource: it creates tension whose meaning depends on the listener's learned expectation that it will resolve.

### Cited Findings
- Plomp & Levelt (1965, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 38:548-560): pure-tone pairs sound consonant when their frequency separation exceeds the critical bandwidth; maximum dissonance (roughness) occurs at roughly a quarter of a critical bandwidth. In Bach and Dvorak chords, the density of simultaneous partials thins out in the bass in line with how critical bandwidth widens (in semitone terms) at low frequencies. -> Do: keep close intervals (2nds, 3rds) out of the low register; voice bass parts in open intervals (octaves, 5ths, 10ths) and put clusters/close voicings in the mid-high range. — [Plomp & Levelt 1965 PDF](https://www.math.miami.edu/~armstrong/592sp15/Plomp_Levelt_1965.pdf); [MPI record](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item66382/tonal-consonance-and-critical-bandwidth)
- McDermott, Lehr & Oxenham (2010, Current Biology 20:1035-1041), >250 listeners: people disliked beating and liked harmonic spectra, but only the harmonicity preference correlated with preference for consonant over dissonant chords; harmonicity preference also grew with years of instrument playing. -> Do: when you want "sweet/stable", choose intervals and voicings whose partials line up (octave, 5th, 4th, major triad in root position, sus chords); when you want "tense", add inharmonic relationships (minor 2nds, tritones, clusters) or inharmonic timbres (bells, FM, detuned metallic sounds). — [McDermott et al. 2010 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2885564); [PDF](https://mcdermottlab.mit.edu/papers/McDermott_Lehr_Oxenham_2010_consonance_individual_differences.pdf)
- McDermott, Schultz, Undurraga & Godoy (2016, Nature): the Tsimane' of Bolivia (minimal Western music exposure) rated consonant and dissonant chords and vocal harmonies as equally pleasant, although they disliked acoustic roughness in other contexts and could discriminate the sounds; Bolivian and US groups with more Western exposure showed progressively stronger consonance preference. -> Do: treat "consonance = pleasant" as a Western-learned convention, not a universal law; it works for Western pop audiences but is a style setting, not physics. — [MIT McGovern summary](https://mcgovern.mit.edu/?p=13810); [Brandeis summary](https://www.brandeis.edu/now/2016/july/music-biology-tsimane.html)
- Bowling, Purves & Gill (2018, PNAS 115(1):216-221): across all chromatic dyads, triads and tetrads within an octave, perceived consonance was predicted by similarity to the harmonic spectra of voiced speech. -> Do: consonant stacks "sound like a voice"; when a part must feel warm and human, favor voicings with harmonic spectral alignment and vocal-like timbres. — [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1292995)
- Huron's expectation theory (Sweet Anticipation, 2006) frames tension as a pre-outcome response to uncertainty and resolution as a prediction reward; common devices such as cadence, tonality and climax exploit this. -> Do: use dissonance as a set-up, not an end state: suspend (4-3, 9-8, 7-6), delay, then resolve on a strong beat. — [Music & Letters review of Sweet Anticipation](https://academic.oup.com/ml/article/89/3/396/1135734); [book listing](https://www.tenlong.com.tw/products/9780262582780)

### Inferences
- Because preference is learned, "tension wants resolution" works because Western listeners have statistically learned that dissonant sonorities are usually followed by consonant ones; the pull is an expectation, which also means it can be subverted for surprise (deceptive resolution).
- Roughness rules are register-dependent: an interval that is fine at C5 can be muddy at C2 because the critical band spans a larger musical interval at low frequencies (follows from Plomp & Levelt). Practical rule of thumb used by arrangers: avoid 3rds below roughly C3 and 2nds below roughly C4 (rule of thumb, not a measured threshold).
- Timbre and harmony interact: a "consonant" chord played with an inharmonic or heavily distorted timbre can sound rough, and vice versa (follows from both the harmonicity and roughness accounts).

### Gaps
- Helmholtz's original roughness theory (On the Sensations of Tone, 1863) was not retrieved; its content is summarized here only via Plomp & Levelt.
- The exact size of the exposure effect on consonance preference across non-Western cultures with harmony (e.g., Balkan, Indonesian traditions) was not researched.
- Later work questioning or refining the Tsimane result (e.g., debates over whether some consonance sensitivity is innate) was not retrieved this session; consider the cultural-learning conclusion strong but not the final word.

## 2. Expectation, prediction and the "sweet spot" of predictability vs surprise

### Takeaway
Pleasure peaks at intermediate complexity: either a surprising event in a context where you felt sure what was coming, or a confirmed event in a context where you were uncertain. Music should establish strong predictions, then violate some of them, and keep enough stability for the brain to keep learning.

### Cited Findings
- Huron's ITPRA theory: five response systems—Imagination and Tension (before the event), Prediction, Reaction and Appraisal (after). Accurate predictions are rewarded in themselves; surprises trigger a fast negative reaction that a slower appraisal can re-evaluate as pleasant ("contrastive valence"). Syncopation, cadences, meter and climax all exploit these systems. -> Do: make the listener able to predict (clear meter, repeated pattern, functional harmony), then place deliberate violations where the slow appraisal will read them as safe and pleasant (e.g., a borrowed chord, a skipped beat before a drop). — [Music & Letters review](https://academic.oup.com/ml/article/89/3/396/1135734); [book summary](https://girlinbluemusic.com/book/sweet-anticipation-music-and-the-psychology-of-expectation-by-david-huron/)
- Cheung, Harrison, Meyer, Pearce, Haynes & Koelsch (2019, Current Biology): a statistical model (IDyOM-type) estimated uncertainty and surprise for ~80,000 chords from US Billboard songs. Pleasure was high for (a) low-uncertainty + high-surprise chords and (b) high-uncertainty + low-surprise chords. Amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex tracked this interaction; nucleus accumbens tracked uncertainty only. -> Do: (a) after a very predictable progression (e.g., three loops of I-V-vi-IV), drop in one unexpected chord (bVII, iv, bVI); (b) after an ambiguous or wandering passage, land on the most expected chord (home tonic). Don't stack surprise on surprise. — [Cambridge publication page](https://cms.mus.cam.ac.uk/publications/cheung-uncertainty-surprise/); [Neuroscience News summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/pop-music-surprise-15181/)
- Gold, Pearce, Mas-Herrero, Dagher & Zatorre (2019, J. Neuroscience): using a computational model of predictability and uncertainty for musical fragments, listeners liked intermediate-complexity fragments most (balance of uncertain expectations and ultimately predictable events), supporting an inverted-U ("Wundt curve") with formal modeling; repeating stimuli lowered liking but did not remove the intermediate-complexity preference. The authors frame musical pleasure as a reward for learning. -> Do: target medium information content per bar; if a section feels boring, add one dimension of novelty (rhythm, harmony, timbre) rather than all at once; if it feels chaotic, increase repetition. — [Gold et al. 2019 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811)
- Matthews et al. (2019, PLOS ONE): for groove, rhythmic complexity followed an inverted U, and rhythm had its strongest effect when paired with medium-complexity chords. -> Do: balance complexity *across* dimensions—busy syncopated rhythm wants simpler or moderately rich harmony, not maximal jazz extensions. — [Matthews et al. 2019 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6328141)

### Inferences
- Statistical learning implies genre matters: "surprise" is defined relative to the listener's genre model. A bVII in pop is mildly surprising; in rock it is a cliché. A composer-AI should set expectation baselines per genre.
- The Cheung result gives a usable two-axis rule: measure how certain the context is; surprise is valuable only when certainty is high, and confirmation is valuable when certainty is low.
- Berlyne's inverted-U (arousal potential vs hedonic value, 1971) is the classical source of the Wundt-curve idea [unverified this session]; Gold 2019 and Cheung 2019 are the modern quantitative support.

### Gaps
- No precise, transferable numeric "optimal surprise" value exists; the models' information-content units are not directly convertible to composition rules.
- Effects of expertise on where the peak of the inverted U sits (musicians may prefer more complexity) were not quantified here.

## 3. Reward, dopamine and chills: why build-ups, drops and peaks work

### Takeaway
Music releases dopamine both during anticipation (caudate) and at the emotional peak (nucleus accumbens). Chills are triggered by structural events that are new, louder/brighter, or harmonically unexpected, and more often by slow, sad, lower-intensity, instrumental music than by matched tracks. Build-ups work because they lengthen and intensify the anticipation phase before a predictable payoff.

### Cited Findings
- Salimpoor, Benovoy, Larcher, Dagher & Zatorre (2011, Nature Neuroscience): PET showed endogenous dopamine release in the striatum at peak emotion; caudate activity was linked to anticipation and nucleus accumbens to the experience of the peak (chills). -> Do: design two reward phases: a clearly signaled lead-in (riser, filter sweep, drum fill, pre-chorus lift) that the listener can predict, then a payoff that delivers. — [Salimpoor et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.zlab.mcgill.ca/publications/docs/salimpoor_2011_nn.pdf); [BRAMS summary](https://brams.org/2011/01/09/salimpoor/)
- Sloboda (1991, Psychology of Music 19:110-120): 83 listeners identified passages producing physical responses; analysis of 38 passages linked tears most reliably to melodic appoggiaturas and sequences; shivers were associated with things like new or unprepared harmonies and sudden dynamic/textural change [the shivers detail is from background knowledge; unverified this session]. Caveat: only "hit" passages were analyzed, so we do not know how often appoggiaturas fail to move people. -> Do: for tear-jerking moments, put a dissonant non-chord tone on a strong beat that resolves stepwise (appoggiatura), especially inside a melodic sequence that builds. — [NPR summary](https://www.npr.org/2012/02/13/146818461/the-ballad-of-the-tearful-why-some-songs-make-you-cry)
- Grewe, Kopiez & Altenmuller (2007, Music Perception 24(3):297-314): chills serve as markers of individual emotional peaks, aligning with peaks in subjective intensity and physiology (skin conductance, heart rate). They also reported chills tend to occur at structural events such as the entry of a voice or new section and loudness increases, but responses were highly individual [trigger details: background knowledge, unverified this session]. -> Do: mark peak moments with a new voice/instrument entering, not just more volume. — [Wicri record](https://wicri-demo.istex.fr/Wicri/Musique/explor/MozartV1/Site/fr/Main/Exploration/bibRecord.php?hk=000D40)
- Same research group (Nagel et al., "Psychoacoustical correlates of musically induced chills", 2008): chill excerpts showed a significant loudness increase specifically in the 8-18 Bark band (about 920-4400 Hz), i.e., the presence/brightness region. -> Do: at climaxes, raise energy in ~1-4.5 kHz (add upper harmonies, open filters, bring vocals/lead forward, add string or synth brightness), not only sub-bass. — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/19439?show=full)
- de Fleurian & Pearce (2021, Psychological Bulletin 147(9):890-920), systematic review: compiled >700 chill-inducing songs; compared to matched tracks by the same artists, chill songs were on average sadder, slower, less intense and more instrumental. -> Do: chills are not only from bombast; sparse, slow, emotionally sad passages with an exposed melody are a reliable vehicle. Contrast (quiet -> swell) matters more than sustained loudness. — [QMRO record](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/handle/123456789/75159); [Quartz summary](https://qz.com/2071652/a-spotify-playlist-with-715-songs-known-to-give-people-chills)

### Inferences
- Why drops work (synthesis of Salimpoor + Huron + Cheung): the build-up raises certainty that a big event is coming (lengthening caudate-type anticipation and Huron's tension), and the drop delivers the predicted event with a big contrast in loudness, bass and texture; a brief silence or "fake-out" before the drop adds a small surprise inside a high-certainty context, matching Cheung's low-uncertainty/high-surprise pleasure zone.
- Crescendos and risers should be predictable in direction but the payoff should add something new (new instrument, new register, harmonic twist), since novelty and new entries are recurring chill triggers.
- Chills are idiosyncratic; no device guarantees them. Design for probability, not certainty.

### Gaps
- Direct experimental evidence on EDM-style build-up/drop structures specifically was not found this session (there is some literature, e.g., on "the drop" in EDM, not retrieved).
- Replication status of Salimpoor 2011's anticipation/peak dissociation was not checked; later pharmacological work (Ferreri et al. 2019, PNAS, dopamine manipulation altering musical pleasure) supports a causal dopamine role [unverified this session].

## 4. Emotion in music: mode, tempo, register, timbre, loudness, and why sad music is enjoyable

### Takeaway
Basic emotions (happy, sad, fearful) are communicated by cues that are partly universal (tempo, loudness, register, articulation) and partly learned (major = happy / minor = sad is strongly culture-dependent). Music induces emotion through several mechanisms (BRECVEMA), so a composer can pull different levers. Sad music is enjoyed because it is "safe" sadness that yields empathy, nostalgia, and aesthetic appreciation.

### Cited Findings
- Fritz et al. (2009, Current Biology): Mafa listeners in Cameroon with no prior exposure to Western music recognized happy, sad and fearful computer-generated piano pieces above chance by matching them to faces. -> Do: tempo, loudness and articulation cues carry emotion across cultures; use them as the primary emotion signal and mode as a secondary, culturally specific one. — [ScienceDaily summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/03/090319132909.htm); [BPS summary](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/emotions-music-are-universally-recognised)
- Juslin (2013, Physics of Life Reviews) BRECVEMA: eight mechanisms by which music induces emotion—Brain stem reflex (sudden loud/dissonant/fast events), Rhythmic entrainment, Evaluative conditioning, Contagion (music imitating emotional voice), Visual imagery, Episodic memory, Musical expectancy, Aesthetic judgment. -> Do: deliberately choose which mechanism a section uses: a sudden hit (brain stem reflex), a steady pulse (entrainment), a crying-voice-like melody with falling contours and slow vibrato (contagion), a nostalgic sound palette (episodic memory/conditioning), expectancy play (section 2), and craftsmanship/virtuosity (aesthetic awe). — [Emotion Researcher article by Juslin's framework](https://emotionresearcher.com/music-and-emotion-there-is-more-to-music-than-meets-the-ear/)
- Sachs, Damasio & Habibi (2015, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience), systematic review: sad music is often experienced as pleasurable; the review attributes this to the aesthetic distance/safe context of the music, along with factors like empathy, mood regulation, nostalgia, and reward-system engagement [the specific factor list is paraphrased from background knowledge of the review]. -> Do: sadness works well when framed by beauty (rich harmony, good production, a resolving cadence) rather than raw unpleasantness; melancholy minor ballads with consonant voicings and lush reverb are a reliable "pleasurable sadness" recipe. — [Sachs et al. 2015 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4513245/)
- de Fleurian & Pearce (2021): chill-inducing songs skew sadder and slower (see section 3), linking sad music to peak pleasure. — [QMRO record](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/handle/123456789/75159)

### Inferences
- Practical cue map (consensus of the music-emotion literature, e.g., Juslin & Laukka 2003 Psychological Bulletin [unverified this session]): happy = faster tempo, major, higher register, staccato/bright, moderate-loud; sad = slow, minor, low register, legato, soft, dark timbre; fear/tension = irregular rhythm, dissonance, sudden dynamics, extreme registers; tenderness = slow, soft, legato, consonant; anger = fast, loud, harsh/distorted timbre, dissonance.
- Mode-emotion pairing should be treated as a strong Western convention; cues like tempo and loudness are the more robust drivers.

### Gaps
- Juslin & Laukka (2003) cue tables, Eerola & Vuoskoski's work on sad music, and Ferreri/Mas-Herrero's dopamine manipulation studies were not retrieved this session.
- Fritz 2009's details on which cues (tempo vs mode) Mafa listeners relied on were not confirmed.

## 5. Repetition, familiarity, loops, hooks and earworms

### Takeaway
Repetition is a defining feature of music, transforming sound into "music" and driving familiarity-based liking; but excessive verbatim repetition of the same stimulus reduces liking. Catchy melodies combine a common, predictable overall contour with a few distinctive intervals/turns, and faster tempi.

### Cited Findings
- Margulis, On Repeat: How Music Plays the Mind (2014, Oxford University Press): repetition itself acts as a musical agent; much of what we perceive as "music" is recognized as such because it repeats. Huron is cited as estimating that more than 90% of music people hear is music they have heard before. -> Do: repeat motifs, riffs and choruses deliberately; repetition is not lazy, it is what makes material feel musical and owned by the listener. — [OUP blog by Margulis](https://blog.oup.com/2014/01/music-repetition-neuroscience/)
- Deutsch, Henthorn & Lapidis (2011, JASA 129:2245-2252), speech-to-song illusion: a spoken phrase repeated ~10 times comes to sound like singing; the effect disappears if intervening repetitions are transposed or the syllables are jumbled. -> Do: exact repetition of a vocal chop or spoken phrase makes it melodic—great for hooks and samples; keep the pitch identical across repeats for this effect. — [Deutsch, UCSD](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/psychology/pages.php?i=212); [ASA abstract page](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/asa/asa156th/deutsch.html)
- Gold et al. (2019, J. Neurosci.): repeating the same stimuli decreased liking ratings, though intermediate complexity remained preferred. -> Do: repeat the hook, but vary its surroundings (arrangement, drums, harmony underneath, register) on each recurrence. — [Gold et al. 2019 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811)
- Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart & Mullensiefen (2017, Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts 11(2):122-135): 100 frequently reported earworm tunes vs 100 matched non-earworm tunes; earworms had faster average tempi, more common global melodic contours (typical rise-and-fall shapes), and less common intervallic patterns between turning points. Popularity also predicted earworm status. -> Do: build hooks on a familiar contour (e.g., arch: rise then fall) but include one distinctive leap or rhythmic quirk; moderate-to-fast tempos help stickiness. — [Goldsmiths research record](https://research.gold.ac.uk/19405); [Goldsmiths news](https://gold.ac.uk/news/scientists-find-key-to-writing-catchy-pop-hits)
- Senn et al. (2018, PLOS ONE): familiarity with a drum pattern positively influenced groove ratings. -> Do: genre-standard grooves get a familiarity bonus; innovate on top of a recognizable groove template rather than abandoning it. — [Senn et al. 2018 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6025871)

### Inferences
- A practical repetition structure that fits both the exposure benefit and the boredom effect: repeat 2- or 4-bar units 2-4 times, then vary (pop's AABA phrase, the 4-bar loop with a turnaround on bar 4 or 8, chorus repeats with added layers).
- Mere exposure effect (Zajonc 1968, liking increases with repeated exposure) underpins hook repetition [unverified this session], but Gold 2019 shows within-session over-repetition can reduce liking: the AI should vary context while keeping the hook constant.

### Gaps
- No reliable source found for a specific number of repetitions before boredom; it is likely style- and complexity-dependent (simpler material saturates faster under the inverted-U account).
- Margulis's own experiments (e.g., adding repetition to modernist pieces increased liking/judged musicality) were not directly retrieved [recalled from background knowledge; unverified].

## 6. Groove: syncopation, bass, microtiming and tempo

### Takeaway
Groove (the pleasurable urge to move) follows an inverted U with syncopation: medium syncopation wins. Bass/low frequencies carry the beat better for both brain and body. Perfect quantization is not penalized and is often rated as groovy or groovier than humanized timing, except for idiomatic swing/shuffle. Preferred tempo clusters around 120-125 BPM (~2 Hz).

### Cited Findings
- Janata, Tomic & Haberman (2012, J. Exp. Psych.: General 141(1):54-75): groove is defined as the urge to move combined with positive affect; listeners agree on which songs groove; high-groove music elicits spontaneous movement and better sensorimotor coupling predicts experienced groove. -> Do: groove is measurable by how easy it is to move along—prioritize a clear, danceable pulse. — [Zenodo record](https://zenodo.org/record/997607)
- Witek, Clarke, Wallentin, Kringelbach & Vuust (2014, PLOS ONE): funk drum breaks with varied syncopation; medium syncopation produced the most desire to move and the most pleasure (inverted U), especially in people who enjoy dancing. -> Do: syncopate some but not all: keep kick on 1 (and often 3) and snare on 2 and 4 as anchors; place syncopation in hats, ghost notes, bass and secondary kicks (e.g., kick on the "and" of 2 or 3). — [Birmingham record](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/syncopation-body-movement-and-pleasure-in-groove-music/); [The Conversation summary](https://theconversation.com/groovy-findings-researching-how-and-why-music-moves-you-112959)
- Matthews et al. (2019, PLOS ONE): rhythm complexity drove pleasure and urge to move with an inverted U; harmony mainly affected the pleasure component; rhythm's effect was strongest with medium-complexity chords. -> Do: pair medium-syncopation beats with 7th-chord-level harmony (not plain triads only, not dense jazz alterations) for maximum groove. — [Matthews et al. 2019 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6328141)
- Senn et al. (2018, PLOS ONE): 248 reconstructed drum patterns, 665 participants, 8,329 ratings: syncopation and event density were positively associated with groove; beat salience, residual microtiming and rhythmic variability had no effect. -> Do: within normal pop ranges, busier (higher event density, e.g., 16th-note hats) and more syncopated patterns groove more; don't rely on random humanization to create groove. — [Senn et al. 2018 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6025871)
- Davies, Madison, Silva & Gouyon (2013, Music Perception): jazz, funk and samba patterns with idiomatic microtiming at 0 to ~2x natural magnitude; systematic microtiming lowered groove, liking and naturalness ratings, except the simple short-long jazz shuffle; fully quantized versions got the highest groove ratings. Contested: this contradicts "participatory discrepancies" theory (Keil), and other studies find style-specific effects. -> Do: default to tight quantization (or very subtle, consistent offsets); use swing/shuffle as a structured, consistent ratio rather than random jitter; large deviations hurt. — [INESC TEC record](https://repositorio.inesctec.pt/handle/123456789/3901?mode=full)
- Lenc, Keller, Varlet & Nozaradan (2018, PNAS 115(32):8221-8226): EEG showed that rhythms carried by low tones (130 Hz) produced selectively stronger neural tracking at the beat frequency than the same rhythms in high tones (1236.8 Hz), especially for syncopated rhythms. -> Do: put the core beat in the low end (kick, bass); when the rhythm is syncopated, the bass can help listeners still lock to the beat. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1801421115); [Neuroscience News summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/bass-music-frequency-9703/)
- Hove, Marie, Bruce & Trainor (2014, PNAS): with two simultaneous tone streams, timing deviations were better detected in the lower stream and tapping was more driven by the lower stream; modeling traced this to the cochlea. Across cultures melody tends to be high and rhythm low. -> Do: tight timing matters most in the bass and kick; lock bass notes to kick hits; melody can carry pitch interest above. — [Hove et al. 2014 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104866)
- Moelants (2002, ICMPC proceedings, "Preferred tempo reconsidered"): DJ BPM lists (>70,000 pieces) and tapping data concentrated around 115-127 BPM with a peak near 125 BPM; preferred tempo range roughly 67-150 BPM; ~2 Hz is the "resonance" region. -> Do: for maximum danceability, sit near 115-130 BPM; slower genres (hip-hop ~85-95) are often felt at double-time hats or half-time snare so a ~2 Hz pulse remains available. — [Frontiers 2022 paper citing Moelants](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952488/pdf)

### Inferences
- The groove "recipe" combines: clear anchors on the grid (predictability), medium syncopation (prediction errors the body "fills in"), dense subdivision layer (hats/shakers), low-frequency beat carriers, and moderate harmony.
- Humanization: evidence suggests timing variation is not what creates groove; velocity/accent variation (dynamics) may matter more [inference; dynamics were not tested in the retrieved studies]. Consistent swing ratios are distinct from random microtiming.
- The ~2 Hz preference is consistent with human locomotion rates (walking ~2 steps/sec) [plausible explanation, not verified this session].

### Gaps
- Stupacher et al.'s work on bass/low frequencies and groove (e.g., bass drum and movement studies) was not retrieved; the low-frequency finding here rests on Lenc 2018 and Hove 2014.
- Studies finding positive microtiming effects in specific styles (e.g., some samba or "laid-back" backbeat studies) were not retrieved to balance Davies 2013; treat microtiming as genuinely contested.
- Moelants 2002 primary text not accessed; numbers come from a secondary paper's summary.

## 7. Melody perception: steps, leaps, contour, and auditory streaming

### Takeaway
Melodies are perceived mostly through pitch proximity (small steps) with occasional leaps that tend to be followed by a change of direction—largely because leaps take you toward the edge of the range. Listeners remember contour easily. The auditory system groups sounds into streams by pitch proximity, timbre, and timing, which is the same principle that separates instruments in a mix.

### Cited Findings
- von Hippel & Huron (2000, Music Perception, "Why do skips precede reversals? The effect of tessitura on melodic structure"): across vocal melodies from four continents, post-skip reversal and gap-fill tendencies are largely explained by regression to the mean—leaps tend to land near the extremes of a melody's range, from which the melody must turn back. -> Do: after a large leap (a 5th or more), move back toward the middle of the melody's range, usually by step; a leap from the center outward should usually be followed by reversal; a leap that returns to center needn't reverse. — [Sonicfield library entry](https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/why-do-skips-precede-reversals-the-effect-of-tessitura-on-melodic-structure); [related learned-heuristics paper](https://lbj.utexas.edu/sites/default/files/file/faculty/Expectations%20as%20learned%20heuristics.pdf)
- Jakubowski et al. (2017): memorable (earworm) melodies use common overall contours with some unusual interval patterns. -> Do: keep the global shape conventional (arch or descending phrase ending) and spice locally. — [Goldsmiths research record](https://research.gold.ac.uk/19405)
- Hove et al. (2014, PNAS): with simultaneous sounds, pitch is better encoded for higher sounds and timing for lower sounds. -> Do: put the melody in the highest prominent voice; it will be heard as the tune. — [Hove et al. 2014 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104866)

### Inferences
- Narmour's implication-realization model (1990) proposes that small intervals imply continuation in the same direction and large intervals imply reversal and a smaller following interval [unverified this session]. Combined with von Hippel & Huron, the practical melodic grammar is: mostly steps, occasional leaps, return after leaps, phrases often ending with descending steps to a stable tone.
- Bregman's Auditory Scene Analysis (1990, MIT Press) principles [unverified this session]: sounds group into one stream when close in pitch, similar in timbre, and in a common onset/fate; fast alternation between distant pitches splits into two streams. Mixing implications: separate instruments by register (different octaves), timbre (bright vs dark), onset timing (don't hit everything on the same 16th), and spatial position (panning); conversely, layer sounds that should fuse with identical onsets, matched envelopes, and harmonic-related pitches.
- Contour memory: listeners, especially non-musicians, retain the up/down shape of a melody more easily than exact intervals (Dowling 1978) [unverified this session] -> a hook's contour should be distinctive at first hearing.

### Gaps
- Primary texts of Narmour, Bregman, and Dowling were not retrieved; the claims above are from background knowledge and flagged.
- Specific numbers on preferred interval sizes (e.g., distribution of step vs leap in folk corpora) were not collected.

## 8. Timbre, loudness, masking and the loudness war

### Takeaway
Instruments clash when their energy falls within the same critical bands at the same time (masking/roughness). Loudness itself does not make records sell or be preferred when level-matched, and hypercompression's side effects are often disliked, though the evidence on listener sensitivity is mixed.

### Cited Findings
- Plomp & Levelt (1965): the critical band is the unit of interaction for roughness; sounds within the same critical band interact strongly. -> Do: avoid stacking sounds with dominant energy in the same band (e.g., kick fundamental vs bass fundamental, vocal presence vs guitar mids); carve with EQ, octave displacement, or sidechain ducking. — [Plomp & Levelt 1965 PDF](https://www.math.miami.edu/~armstrong/592sp15/Plomp_Levelt_1965.pdf)
- Vickers (2010, AES 129th Convention; shorter version J. AES 2011), "The Loudness War: Background, Speculation and Recommendations": reviewed evidence that loudness does not correlate with sales, has little effect on listener preference between songs, that listeners tend to dislike the side effects of hypercompression and prefer more dynamic music, and discussed listening fatigue. -> Do: master to streaming loudness norms rather than maximizing loudness; preserve transients and section-to-section dynamics (verse quieter than chorus) because contrast drives peak moments. — [AES e-library](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=15598); [CDM summary](https://cdm.link/from-the-trenches-of-the-loudness-wars-a-broad-survey-of-research/)
- Mixed evidence: an Aalborg University study of original vs more-compressed remasters of pop recordings found no evidence that dynamic range compression affected subjective preference or perceived depth, suggesting listeners are less sensitive to compression than often assumed; other work (e.g., a Goldsmiths SysMus 2014 study) found increasing compression significantly lowered audio quality judgments. -> Do: moderate compression is not a problem; heavy limiting that audibly squashes transients and removes dynamic contrast is the risk. — [Aalborg record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/perceptual-effects-of-dynamic-range-compression-in-popular-music-); [Goldsmiths SysMus14](https://bjmh.gold.ac.uk/index.php/sysmus14/article/view/236)
- Nagel/Grewe group (2008): chills associated with loudness increases in ~920-4400 Hz (see section 3). -> Do: dynamic contrast in the presence range is an emotional tool; a mix that is already maxed there has no room to climax. — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/19439?show=full)

### Inferences
- Louder-sounds-better bias in A/B comparisons (equal-loudness effects) means any evaluation of mix decisions should be loudness-matched [standard audio-engineering practice, not sourced this session].
- Since streaming services normalize loudness, a hypercompressed master gets turned down and loses punch relative to dynamic masters [widely reported; not verified with a primary source this session].

### Gaps
- Primary psychoacoustics references on simultaneous masking (e.g., Zwicker & Fastl, Moore) were not retrieved; masking claims rely on Plomp & Levelt's critical-band framing and standard practice.
- Spotify/Apple/YouTube loudness normalization targets (around -14 LUFS for several services) were not verified this session.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that quantifies an ideal dynamic range (e.g., PLR/LRA values) for listener preference by genre.

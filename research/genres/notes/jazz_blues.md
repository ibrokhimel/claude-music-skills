## 1. What forms, chord vocabulary and scales define each genre? (Genre recipe cards)

### Takeaway
Blues genres share the 12-bar I–IV–V dominant-7th form and the blues scale and differ mainly in instrumentation, amplification and groove (Delta = solo acoustic/slide and loose bar counts; Chicago = amplified band shuffle; jump = horn band + boogie bass). Jazz styles differ by tempo, harmonic rhythm and drum role: New Orleans (two-beat, collective improvisation) → swing (4/4 walking bass, big-band sections) → bebop (fast, ii–V/tritone-sub density, ride-cymbal time) → cool (soft, arranged) / hard bop (bluesy, gospel) → modal (one chord for many bars, Dorian, quartal voicings) → fusion/smooth/acid/nu-jazz/UK jazz (straight 16ths, electric keys, funk/house/afrobeat/broken-beat grooves).

#### Shared harmonic toolkit (applies to all cards below)
- **12-bar blues (convention)**: `| I7 | IV7 | I7 | I7 | IV7 | IV7 | I7 | I7 | V7 | IV7 | I7 | V7 (turnaround) |` — in A: `A7 D7 A7 A7 | D7 D7 A7 A7 | E7 D7 A7 E7`. Bar 2 IV7 = "quick change"; slow change keeps I7 for bars 1–4 (convention).
- **8-bar blues (convention)**: `| I | V | IV | IV | I | V | I | V |` ("Key to the Highway" type) [BK]. **16-bar blues**: 12-bar with bars 9–10 extended (e.g. "Watermelon Man", Herbie Hancock 1962) [BK].
- **Jazz blues in F (convention)**: `| F7 | Bb7 | F7 | Cm7 F7 | Bb7 | Bdim7 | F7 | Am7 D7 | Gm7 | C7 | F7 D7 | Gm7 C7 |`. **Bird/"Blues for Alice" blues (convention)**: `| Fmaj7 | Em7b5 A7 | Dm7 G7 | Cm7 F7 | Bb7 | Bbm7 Eb7 | Am7 D7 | Abm7 Db7 | Gm7 | C7 | F D7 | Gm7 C7 |` (descending ii–Vs). **Minor blues in C (convention)**: `| Cm7 | Fm7 | Cm7 | Cm7 | Fm7 | Fm7 | Cm7 | Cm7 | Ab7 | G7alt | Cm7 | Dm7b5 G7 |`.
- **Rhythm changes (32-bar AABA) in Bb (convention)**: A = `| Bbmaj7 G7 | Cm7 F7 | Dm7 G7 | Cm7 F7 | Fm7 Bb7 | Ebmaj7 Ab7 | Dm7 G7 | Cm7 F7 |` (A2 ends on Bb); bridge = `| D7 | D7 | G7 | G7 | C7 | C7 | F7 | F7 |`. Bebop used rhythm changes (I–vi–ii–V) and contrafacts — new melodies on old swing-era changes — as core repertoire — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop).
- **ii–V–I and tritone sub (convention)**: C major: `Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7`; tritone sub `Dm7 – Db7 – Cmaj7` (Db7 shares the guide tones F/B=Cb with G7). Bebop is characterised by tritone substitutions, altered chords and emphasis on upper extensions — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop).
- **Guide tones**: the 3rd and 7th define chord quality and move by step/common tone through ii–V–I; the 5th is usually omitted — [The Jazz Resource: Shell Voicings](https://www.thejazzresource.com/shell_voicings.html); [Bass Musician Magazine: Guide Tones](https://bassmusicianmagazine.com/2012/08/guide-tones-by-joshua-barnhart/).
- **Scales (convention)**: blues scale 1 b3 4 #4 5 b7 (C: C Eb F F# G Bb); major blues 1 2 b3 3 5 6 (C: C D Eb E G A); bebop dominant = Mixolydian + major 7 passing tone (C D E F G A Bb B) so chord tones land on downbeats; bebop major = major + #5 (C D E F G G# A B); Dorian (minor ii and modal jazz); altered scale (7th mode of melodic minor) over V7alt; half-whole diminished over 7b9; Lydian dominant over tritone subs/non-resolving 7ths.

#### Card: Delta blues
1. **Identity**: early-20th-c. Mississippi Delta; first recorded in the late 1920s/Depression era; Freddie Spruell "Milk Cow Blues" (1926) earliest known recording; artists Charley Patton (rec. 1929–34), Son House (from 1930), Robert Johnson (1936–37), Tommy Johnson, Memphis Minnie, Geeshie Wiley — [Wikipedia: Delta blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_blues). Refs [BK]: Patton "Pony Blues" (1929), Robert Johnson "Cross Road Blues" (1936), Son House "Death Letter" (1965 re-recording), Skip James "Devil Got My Woman" (1931). Ear test: one voice + one acoustic guitar (often slide), droning bass, loose irregular bar lengths.
2. **Tempo & feel**: 60–110 BPM, often drifting (convention); triplet/shuffle feel or loose straight; 12/8 thinking.
3. **Harmony**: I7–IV7–V7 but often one chord drone; bars added/dropped to follow the vocal (12½, 13, 14-bar choruses) (convention). Open tunings — open G (D G D G B D) and open D (D A D F# A D) — and Skip James' D-minor tuning [BK].
4. **Melody & vocals**: blues scale with bends between b3 and 3 and b7 (blue notes are pitch zones, not fixed notes); falsetto breaks, moans, hummed lines; guitar answers the voice (call-and-response) (convention). Vocals range from introspective to fiery — [Wikipedia: Delta blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_blues).
5. **Instrumentation**: acoustic guitar and harmonica dominate; slide guitar is a hallmark — [Wikipedia: Delta blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_blues). DAW: resonator/steel-string acoustic sample library with slide articulations; pitch-bend range ±2–12 semitones for slides; foot-stomp sample (convention).
6. **Rhythm** (12-step, one bar): `Stomp X . . | X . . | X . . | X . . ` / `Gtr bass X . x | X . x | X . x | X . x` (thumb on root with shuffle pickup) (convention).
7. **Bass**: no bassist — guitar thumb alternates/drones on the low root (convention).
8. **Form**: verse = AAB lyric over a (loose) 12-bar; 2.5–3.5 min 78-rpm length (convention).
9. **Mix**: mono, narrow-band, noisy 78 transfer, room sound; no reverb added (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: Piedmont blues = fingerpicked ragtime-like alternating bass, lighter; hill country blues (R.L. Burnside) = one-chord trance grooves [BK].
11. **FL Studio**: record/draw guitar with heavy pitch-bend slides into notes; leave tempo free or automate ±3 BPM drift; avoid perfect 12-bar grids; filter 150 Hz–5 kHz + vinyl crackle for "78" vibe. Fake tells: quantized strumming, fixed b3 with no bends, a drum kit.

#### Card: Chicago (electric) blues
1. **Identity**: evolved from Delta blues during the Great Migration; Muddy Waters ("father" of Chicago blues), Howlin' Wolf, Willie Dixon, John Lee Hooker; Chess and Vee-Jay labels; international by late 1950s–early 60s; West Side = smoother/jazzier, South Side = rawer — [Wikipedia: Chicago blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_blues). Refs [BK]: Muddy Waters "Hoochie Coochie Man" (1954, stop-time riff), Little Walter "Juke" (1952), Howlin' Wolf "Smokestack Lightnin'" (one-chord), Elmore James "Dust My Broom" (triplet slide riff). Ear test: overdriven electric guitar + amplified harmonica over a full band shuffle.
2. **Tempo & feel**: slow blues 50–75 BPM in 12/8; shuffles 100–150 BPM (convention). Heavy triplet shuffle (~2:1–3:1).
3. **Harmony**: 12-bar I7–IV7–V7 (quick change common); one-chord vamps; stop-time riffs (band hits beat 1, rests) (convention). Harmonica in 2nd position/"cross harp" (key A on a D harp) (convention).
4. **Melody**: minor-pentatonic/blues-scale licks with string bends, vibrato, slide; vocal shouted, gritty; call-and-response with harp/guitar (convention).
5. **Instrumentation**: solid-body electric guitar and harmonica amplified through PA/guitar amps, plus drums, bass (upright then electric) and piano; amplification produced distortion — [Wikipedia: Chicago blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_blues). DAW: clean electric guitar → amp sim (small tube combo, edge of breakup); harmonica sample through bandpass (300 Hz–3 kHz) + overdrive (convention).
6. **Rhythm** (12-step shuffle): `Snare . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .` / `HH/ride x . x | x . x | x . x | x . x` / `Kick X . . | x . . | X . . | x . .` (four on the floor, soft on 2/4) (convention). Guitar "boogie shuffle" in A, long-short: A5–A6–A5–A6... i.e. root+5th alternating with root+6th, and root+b7 at peak (convention).
7. **Bass**: walking boogie (1–3–5–6–b7–6–5–3) or root–5–6–5 shuffle, doubling the guitar riff (convention).
8. **Form**: intro (often V turnaround) → 3–6 vocal choruses → guitar/harp solo choruses → vocal → turnaround ending; 3–4 min (convention).
9. **Mix**: mono 1950s Chess sound, room/slapback, amp grit; modern: wider, cleaner (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: Texas blues = jazzier, swingier lead (T-Bone Walker, SRV); British blues/blues rock = louder, rock drums [BK].
11. **FL Studio**: program shuffle on 12-step/triplet piano-roll snap; keep kick soft (vel ~60) and snare backbeat 100+; harp and guitar trade fills in the 2-bar gaps after each vocal line. Fake tells: straight 8ths, clean DI guitar, no bends.

#### Card: Jump blues
1. **Identity**: late 1930s–early 40s, peak late 40s–early 50s; Louis Jordan (most popular), Lionel Hampton, Roy Brown, Wynonie Harris, Big Jay McNeely, Earl Bostic, Amos Milburn; precursor to R&B and rock and roll; refs "Saturday Night Fish Fry", "Flying Home", "Good Rockin' Tonight" — [Wikipedia: Jump blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_blues). Ear test: small horn band, honking tenor sax, boogie bass, humorous shouted vocals, danceable shuffle.
2. **Tempo & feel**: uptempo/"bright bounce" — [Wikipedia: Jump blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_blues); 140–200 BPM (convention); hard shuffle, backbeat on 2 & 4.
3. **Harmony**: 12-bar blues (I7–IV7–V7, 6th chords), riff tunes; occasional AABA 32-bar pop forms (convention).
4. **Melody & vocals**: playful jive-talk lyrics, call-and-response between singer and band — [Wikipedia: Jump blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_blues); honking/screaming tenor solos (convention).
5. **Instrumentation**: 5–7 pieces: saxophones, (trumpet), piano/guitar, bass, drums; short horn riffs — [Wikipedia: Jump blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_blues). DAW: tenor/alto/bari sax library with growl articulations; upright bass slap; bright piano.
6. **Rhythm** (12-step): `Snare . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .` / `Ride x . x | x . x | x . x | x . x` / `Kick X . . | . . . | X . . | . . .`; piano LH eight-to-the-bar boogie (all 8 swung 8ths) — boogie-woogie "eight-to-the-bar" and shuffle rhythm are defining — [Wikipedia: Jump blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_blues).
7. **Bass**: boogie walking 1–3–5–6–b7–6–5–3 in swung 8ths or quarters (convention).
8. **Form**: intro riff → vocal choruses with horn backgrounds → sax solo (often 2–4 choruses) → stop-time breaks → vocal → tag; 2.5–3 min (convention).
9. **Mix**: mono, horns forward, slap-back; loud for jukeboxes (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: swing big band = bigger, less blues-shouting; early R&B/rock and roll = backbeat heavier, electric guitar forward [BK].
11. **FL Studio**: write a 2-bar unison horn riff, repeat behind vocals, harmonise in 3 parts on the last chorus; boogie bass + piano LH doubled. Fake tells: horn riffs with no scoops/falls, kick too loud.

#### Card: Ragtime
1. **Identity**: early 1890s African-American communities of the Midwest/South; peak 1890s–1910s; Scott Joplin "Maple Leaf Rag" (1899), "The Entertainer" (1902); 1970s revival via *The Sting* (1973) — [Wikipedia: Ragtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime). Ear test: solo piano, oom-pah left hand, syncopated right-hand against a march pulse, straight (unswung) rhythm.
2. **Tempo & feel**: 2/4 or 4/4; Joplin: never play ragtime fast — [Wikipedia: Ragtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime); ~70–100 quarter-note BPM in 2/4 (convention). Straight 16ths (convention).
3. **Harmony**: functional major-key triads/7ths, secondary dominants (V/V, V/ii), diminished passing chords; trio section modulates to IV (convention).
4. **Melody**: syncopation defines it — accents between beats — [Wikipedia: Ragtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime); 16th–8th–16th "cakewalk" cell, broken octaves (convention).
5. **Instrumentation**: solo piano (also band arrangements). DAW: upright/honky-tonk piano (slight detune) or FL Keys piano (convention).
6. **Rhythm** (16 steps = 2 bars of 2/4): `LH bass X . . . | x . . . | X . . . | x . . .` / `LH chord . . x . | . . x . | . . x . | . . x .` (bass on 1/3, chords on 2/4 in 8ths — [Wikipedia: Ragtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime)) / `RH X x . x | x . x . | X x . x | . x . .` (cakewalk syncopation, convention).
7. **Bass**: LH alternates root octave and 5th/other chord tone on strong 8ths (convention).
8. **Form**: multiple 16-bar strains, e.g. AABBACCDD / AABBCCDD — [Wikipedia: Ragtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime); ~3 min.
9. **Mix**: dry piano, light room; for vintage use bandpass + noise (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: stride piano (1920s Harlem: James P. Johnson, Fats Waller) = swung, faster, wider LH leaps; novelty piano [BK].
11. **FL Studio**: piano roll, snap 1/4 step; velocity LH bass 80–95, LH chords 55–70, RH melody 85–105; no swing. Fake tells: swinging it, playing too fast, perfectly even velocity.

#### Card: New Orleans / Dixieland
1. **Identity**: New Orleans, start of the 20th c.; Original Dixieland Jass Band 1917 = first jazz recordings; King Oliver, Louis Armstrong, Jelly Roll Morton; Chicago style (1920s) faster with more solos; West Coast revival late 1930s — [Wikipedia: Dixieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixieland). Refs [BK]: King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band "Dipper Mouth Blues" (1923), Armstrong Hot Five "West End Blues" (1928), Preservation Hall Jazz Band (living tradition). Ear test: trumpet lead + clarinet weaving above + trombone smears below, all improvising at once, over a two-beat tuba/banjo.
2. **Tempo & feel**: two-beat feel — [Wikipedia: Dixieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixieland); 100–220 BPM (convention); lighter, "lilting" swing (~1.5:1–2:1) (convention).
3. **Harmony**: major-key triads/dominant 7ths, circle-of-5ths secondary dominants (III7–VI7–II7–V7), 12-bar blues and multi-strain marches; 8–16-bar strains (convention).
4. **Melody**: trumpet paraphrases melody, other front-line instruments improvise around it (polyphonic collective improvisation) — [Wikipedia: Dixieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixieland); clarinet arpeggio runs, trombone glissandi/"tailgate" smears, trumpet shakes (convention).
5. **Instrumentation**: front line trumpet/cornet, clarinet, trombone; rhythm tuba or string bass, banjo or guitar, piano, drums — [Wikipedia: Dixieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixieland). DAW: brass/woodwind solo libraries with legato + glide; banjo sample; tuba.
6. **Rhythm** (12-step): `Tuba X . . | . . . | X . . | . . .` / `Banjo x . . | X . . | x . . | X . .` / `Snare (press-roll/backbeat) . . . | x . . | . . . | x . g` / `Woodblock/cymbal x . x | x . x | x . x | x . x` (convention). Second-line parade variant: syncopated bass drum + snare rolls (convention).
7. **Bass**: tuba/bass on beats 1 and 3, root and 5th (convention).
8. **Form**: ensemble chorus → solo breaks/stop-time "breaks" → solo choruses → out-chorus collective improvisation with drum "tag" ending; 3 min (convention).
9. **Mix**: 1920s acoustic recordings — mono, no low end, horn-dominant; modern trad = natural room (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: Chicago-style Dixieland = faster, 4/4, more solos; modern brass band (Rebirth, Dirty Dozen) = sousaphone funk grooves [BK].
11. **FL Studio**: write three independent lines in contrary motion (trumpet mid, clarinet high/busy, trombone low/sparse), avoid parallel harmony; tuba on 1 & 3. Fake tells: horns in block chords, walking 4/4 bass (that's swing), heavy reverb.

#### Card: Swing / big band
1. **Identity**: developed late 1920s–early 30s, golden age 1935–46; Count Basie (Kansas City swing: blues riffs), Duke Ellington, Benny Goodman, Glenn Miller ("sweet"), Fletcher Henderson; revivals incl. 1990s neo-swing and electro swing — [Wikipedia: Swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music). Refs [BK]: Basie "One O'Clock Jump" (1937), Ellington "Take the 'A' Train" (1941), Goodman "Sing, Sing, Sing" (1937), Miller "In the Mood" (1939). Ear test: saxes and brass trading riffs over a steady 4/4 walking bass and chunking guitar.
2. **Tempo & feel**: 4/4 with walking bass replacing two-beat — [Wikipedia: Swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music); dance tempos 120–220 BPM, ballads 60–80 (convention); swung 8ths (see Q2 for ratio by tempo).
3. **Harmony**: 6th and 7th chords (Cmaj6, C6/9, dominant 9/13), ii–V, 12-bar blues and 32-bar AABA songs; diminished passing chords (convention).
4. **Melody**: riff-based tunes; call-and-response between brass and reed sections — [Wikipedia: Swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music); crooned vocals.
5. **Instrumentation**: sax, trumpet, trombone sections + rhythm (bass, drums, piano, guitar) — [Wikipedia: Swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music). Standard 17-piece: 5 saxes (2 alto, 2 tenor, bari), 4 trumpets, 4 trombones, 4 rhythm (convention). DAW: big-band section libraries (sections, falls, doits, shakes), or solo instruments stacked.
6. **Rhythm** (12-step): `Ride/HH x . . | X . x | x . . | X . x` / `HH foot . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .` / `Kick g . . | g . . | g . . | g . .` (feathered 4-on-floor) / `Guitar x . . | x . . | x . . | x . .` (Freddie Green four-to-the-bar, short) (convention).
7. **Bass**: walking quarter notes (see Q2).
8. **Form**: intro → head (AABA 32 / 12-bar blues) → solos with section backgrounds → shout chorus → recap/tag ending; 3 min (78 era) (convention).
9. **Mix**: room-heavy, sections spread across stereo in modern recordings (saxes front, trombones mid, trumpets back/higher), minimal compression (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: Kansas City = bluesier, riff-head jams; sweet bands = lusher, less improvisation; electro swing = sampled swing over house beats (~120–128 BPM) (convention).
11. **FL Studio**: see Q3 (voicing/arranging). Fake tells: sections perfectly aligned to grid (real sections smear ~10–20 ms), no falls/doits, kick on every beat at loud velocity.

#### Card: Bebop
1. **Identity**: early-to-mid 1940s, mainly New York, grew out of swing/Kansas City; Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Bud Powell, Thelonious Monk, Max Roach, Kenny Clarke; first formal bebop session Coleman Hawkins, 16 Feb 1944 ("Woody'n You") — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop). Refs [BK]: Parker "Ko-Ko" (1945), "Donna Lee", Gillespie "Salt Peanuts", "A Night in Tunisia", Powell "Un Poco Loco". Ear test: fast unison sax+trumpet head full of 8th-note chromaticism, then long solos over rapidly changing chords; drums keep time on ride and drop "bombs".
2. **Tempo & feel**: fast, not for dancing — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop); 180–320+ BPM typical, ballads 50–70 (convention). At fast tempos swing ratio approaches 1:1 — [Friberg & Sundström / ASA](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/137th/friberg.html).
3. **Harmony**: rhythm changes, contrafacts, tritone subs, altered chords, upper extensions (b9, #9, #11, b13) — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop); harmonic rhythm 1–2 chords/bar; Bird blues (see toolkit).
4. **Melody**: continuous 8th-note lines from bebop scales, chromatic enclosures (approach target from half-step above and below), triplet turns, lines starting on offbeats, ending on beat-2-and accents ("be-BOP" two-note endings) (convention).
5. **Instrumentation**: small combo 5–6 players replacing big bands; alto/tenor sax, trumpet, piano, bass, drums — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop).
6. **Rhythm** (12-step): `Ride x . . | X . x | x . . | X . x` / `HH foot . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .` / `Snare comp . . g | . . . | . . x | . . .` (vary every bar) / `Kick . . . | . . . | . . . | . . X` (occasional "bomb") — ride as primary timekeeper, bombs responding to soloists — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop).
7. **Bass**: walking quarters, chromatic approach on beat 4, at 250+ BPM still quarters (convention).
8. **Form**: intro (often 4–8 bars) → head (AABA 32 or 12-bar blues) x1–2 → solos (several choruses each) → trading fours with drums → head → tag/coda (convention).
9. **Mix**: 1940s mono, saxophone forward; modern = natural acoustic, light compression (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: hard bop = slower, bluesier, gospel; cool = softer tone, arranged; post-bop = modal/free harmony (convention).
11. **FL Studio**: write heads in unison sax+trumpet an octave apart; at 240+ BPM set swing near 1.4:1 (see Q2); piano comps sparse rootless voicings on offbeats. Fake tells: 2:1 triplet swing at 300 BPM, comping on every beat, bass playing roots only.

#### Card: Cool jazz (incl. West Coast)
1. **Identity** [BK — Cool jazz page not fetched in this pass]: Miles Davis *Birth of the Cool* (recorded 1949–50, Gil Evans/Gerry Mulligan arrangements), Chet Baker, Gerry Mulligan pianoless quartet, Lee Konitz, Lennie Tristano, Stan Getz, Modern Jazz Quartet, Dave Brubeck Quartet "Take Five" (1959, 5/4, Paul Desmond). Ear test: soft, vibrato-light tone, relaxed medium tempo, contrapuntal arranged lines, brushes.
2. **Tempo & feel**: 90–180 BPM (convention); light 2:1-ish swing; odd meters in Brubeck (5/4, 9/8) [BK].
3. **Harmony**: same ii–V vocabulary as bebop but lighter density; maj7/9 colours; counterpoint over changes (convention).
4. **Melody**: lyrical, mid-register, fewer notes, understated; counterpoint between two horns (convention).
5. **Instrumentation**: trumpet/alto/baritone sax, sometimes French horn/tuba (nonet), piano or pianoless, brushes on snare (convention).
6. **Rhythm** (12-step, brushes): `Snare brush sweep X ~ ~ | x ~ ~ | X ~ ~ | x ~ ~` (continuous circular sweep, accent 2&4 with taps) / `Ride x . . | x . x | x . . | x . x` (soft) / `HH foot . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .` (convention; `~` = sustained sweep).
7. **Bass**: walking, often two-feel in heads (convention).
8. **Form**: AABA/blues heads, short solos, arranged interludes, contrapuntal out-heads (convention).
9. **Mix**: transparent, warm, not loud; West Coast studio (Contemporary/Pacific Jazz) clarity (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: vs bebop = slower, softer, arranged; vs hard bop = less blues/gospel, lighter drums.
11. **FL Studio**: brushes loop (swept snare sample) + light ride; horns with low velocity/soft dynamic layers; two independent horn lines. Fake tells: sticks instead of brushes, bright aggressive horn samples.

#### Card: Hard bop (incl. soul jazz)
1. **Identity**: mid-1950s New York and Detroit; Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers credited originators; Horace Silver, Clifford Brown, Lee Morgan, Cannonball Adderley, Sonny Rollins, Coltrane; key records Silver "The Preacher" (1954), *A Night at Birdland* (1954), *Moanin'* (1958), Coltrane *Blue Train* — [Wikipedia: Hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop). Refs [BK]: Lee Morgan "The Sidewinder" (1964), Silver "Song for My Father" (1965). Ear test: bluesy, gospel "amen" riffs, punchy drums, horn heads that are catchy and riff-like.
2. **Tempo & feel**: medium-up swing 120–240 BPM; also boogaloo/straight-8th grooves (Sidewinder), Latin-tinged heads (convention). Repetitive, dance-like rhythms — [Wikipedia: Hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop).
3. **Harmony**: blues, minor blues, ii–Vs, gospel plagal cadences (IV–I, "amen") — [Wikipedia: Hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop); minor-key tunes with m6/m9 colours (convention).
4. **Melody**: simpler, earthy, bluesy melodies — [Wikipedia: Hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop); call-and-response heads (Moanin': piano call, horns answer) [BK].
5. **Instrumentation**: trumpet + tenor sax quintet, piano, bass, drums; Hammond B3 organ trio in soul jazz (Jimmy Smith) [BK].
6. **Rhythm** (12-step): `Ride x . . | X . x | x . . | X . x` / `HH foot . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .` / `Snare . . . | . . g | . . . | x . X` (more aggressive comping, press roll at chorus ends — Blakey) (convention).
7. **Bass**: walking, more solo space for bassists — [Wikipedia: Hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop).
8. **Form**: head–solos–head, often with written shout/send-off figures and drum features (convention).
9. **Mix**: Rudy Van Gelder / Blue Note sound — close, punchy horns, prominent ride, short room [BK].
10. **Neighbours**: soul jazz = organ, more groove, shorter solos; bebop = faster/more abstract.
11. **FL Studio**: write a 2-bar call-and-response head, use minor blues or plagal cadences; Rhodes/organ option for soul jazz. Fake tells: too polite drums, no blues inflection.

#### Card: Modal jazz
1. **Identity**: mid-1950s US; George Russell key theorist; Miles Davis "Milestones" (1958) and *Kind of Blue* (1959); Coltrane quartet (1960–65) with McCoy Tyner; Herbie Hancock, Bill Evans, Wayne Shorter — [Wikipedia: Modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz). Refs [BK]: "So What", Coltrane "Impressions", Hancock "Maiden Voyage" (1965). Ear test: one chord for 8–16 bars, bass ostinato/pedal, quartal piano chords, scalar improvisation.
2. **Tempo & feel**: medium swing (~130–150 "So What" range) to very fast (Coltrane "Impressions") (convention); Elvin Jones-style triplet polyrhythm over swing (convention).
3. **Harmony**: modes rather than chord sequences; slow harmonic rhythm; pedal tonic in bass; "So What" = AABA with D Dorian A sections and Eb Dorian bridge — [Wikipedia: Modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz) — i.e. 32 bars: 16 Dm7, 8 Ebm7, 8 Dm7 (derived). "So What chord" (convention): stacked 4ths + major 3rd, e.g. E–A–D–G–B over D. Sus chords (Maiden Voyage: D7sus4 / F7sus4 / Eb7sus4 / Db7sus4) [BK].
4. **Melody**: Dorian/Mixolydian/Lydian scalar lines, pentatonic and quartal patterns, sequence/sheets of sound (convention).
5. **Instrumentation**: trumpet/tenor, piano, upright bass, drums. Quartal "chords voiced in fourths" (McCoy Tyner) — [Wikipedia: Modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz).
6. **Rhythm** (12-step): swing ride as bebop; plus 6/8 / "3 over 4" cross-rhythm: triplet-quarter "3 over 2" cross-rhythm (every 2nd triplet-8th = steps 1,3,5,7,9,11): `Tom/snare X . x | . x . | X . x | . x .` (convention). (A dotted-quarter pulse cannot be drawn on a 12-step grid; use 1/2-beat snap in a 16-step grid: steps 1, 7, 13.)
7. **Bass**: ostinato riff answering piano ("So What" call-and-response), or walking around the mode tonic (convention).
8. **Form**: AABA 32 modal head or open vamps, very long solos, piano interludes (convention).
9. **Mix**: natural, spacious 1959 Columbia sound (30th Street Studio) [BK].
10. **Neighbours**: post-bop (Shorter, Hancock 60s) = modal + non-functional changes; spiritual jazz (Pharoah Sanders, Alice Coltrane) = drones, harp, long vamps [BK].
11. **FL Studio**: pick a mode, hold one chord 8–16 bars, voice piano in 4ths moving in parallel within the mode; bass pedal or ostinato. Fake tells: ii–V clichés over the vamp, root-position triads.

#### Card: Latin jazz (Afro-Cuban + Brazilian/bossa)

Ear test: straight 8ths, clave/cross-stick pattern, congas/bongos (Cuban) or nylon guitar + soft voice (bossa).
2. **Tempo & feel**: straight (no swing). 5. **Instrumentation**: Cuban = congas, bongos, timbales, cowbell, guiro, piano montuno, bass tumbao, horns; bossa = nylon guitar, brushes/cross-stick, upright bass, piano, flute/sax (convention).

One-bar 16-step versions (derived/convention):
   `Son clave 3-2  X . . X | . . X . | . . X . | X . . .`
   `Rumba clave    X . . X | . . . X | . . X . | X . . .`
   `Bossa clave    X . . X | . . X . | . . X . | . X . .`
   Bossa drum-set (one 4/4 bar, 16 steps): `Kick X . . . | . . X . | X . . . | . . X .` / `HH/shaker x x x x | x x x x | x x x x | x x x x` (straight 8ths/16ths, soft) / `Cross-stick` = bossa clave spread over 2 bars: bar1 steps 1,7,13; bar2 steps 5,11 (derived). Conga tumbao: `. . . . | . . g . | . . . . | . . X X` (open tones on 4 and 4-and; slaps vary) (convention).
7. **Bass**: Afro-Cuban tumbao = anticipated, no downbeat: notes on "and of 2" (step 7) and beat 4 (step 13), tied over (convention). Bossa = root on 1, 5th on "and of 2"/beat 3 (half-note dotted pattern matching kick) (convention).
8. **Form**: AABA songs (Ipanema 40 bars: A8 A8 B16 A8) [BK]; Afro-Cuban: head → montuno vamp solos → mambo horn section → coda (convention).
9. **Mix**: bossa = intimate, close vocal, dry-ish; Afro-Cuban = percussion panned wide (congas one side, bongos/timbales other) (convention).
10. **Neighbours**: samba-jazz = faster (~200+), busier 16ths; boogaloo = Latin + soul backbeat; Latin jazz vs salsa = more improvisation, jazz harmony (convention).
11. **FL Studio**: zero swing; program clave first, align bass/piano accents to it (3-side vs 2-side); bossa: nylon guitar comping on clave-like 16ths with thumb bass on 1 and 3. Fake tells: swinging bossa, clave reversed against the melody, congas at constant velocity.

## 2. How does swing feel work (ratios by tempo, ride pattern, hi-hat 2&4, walking bass, comping, shuffle)?

### Takeaway
Swing ratio is not fixed at 2:1: drummers' ride ratios run up to ~3.5:1 at slow tempos down to ~1:1 at fast tempos, with the short note staying ~100 ms at medium-fast tempos. Program swing as a tempo-dependent offbeat position, with ride on all 4 beats + skip notes, hi-hat foot on 2 & 4, feathered kick, walking bass quarters and sparse syncopated comping.

### Cited Findings
- Friberg & Sundström (KTH) measured ride-cymbal swing ratios of Tony Williams, Jack DeJohnette, Jeff Watts and Adam Nussbaum on commercial records: ratio "almost 3.5" at slow tempi falling to ~1:1 at fast tempi; 2:1 "triple feel" occurs only at a certain tempo; the short note is ~100 ms constant for medium-to-fast tempi (exception: DeJohnette at 72 BPM short note 416 ms) — [ASA lay-language paper: Friberg](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/137th/friberg.html).
- Hi-hat (foot) on beats 2 and 4 with the ride pattern is the basic jazz pattern — [Free Drum Lessons / Drumeo basic jazz pattern](https://freedrumlessons.com/drum-lessons/basic-jazz-pattern.php); [Vic Firth: Swingin' Easy PDF](https://ae.vicfirth.com/wp-content/uploads/FADrumset-swingin-easy.pdf).
- "Feathering" the bass drum = very soft quarter notes with the bass, felt not heard; if you can hear it in the band it's too loud — [Cruise Ship Drummer: feathering](https://cruiseshipdrummer.com/?p=796); [Vic Firth PDF](https://ae.vicfirth.com/wp-content/uploads/FADrumset-swingin-easy.pdf).
- In jazz the ride cymbal, not the hi-hat, is the dominant timekeeper; program cymbal parts in real time with a velocity-sensitive controller where possible — [Sound On Sound: Effective drum programming pt 2](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/effective-drum-programming-part-2).
- Bebop moved timekeeping to the ride and introduced kick "bombs" — [Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop). Swing era moved from two-beat to 4/4 walking bass — [Wikipedia: Swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music).

#### Swing-ratio programming table (derived)
Ratio r:1 puts the offbeat 8th at r/(r+1) of the beat; with short note ≈100 ms (medium–fast), r ≈ (60000/BPM − 100)/100, capped ~3.5 at slow tempi (sourced cap). FL Studio ticks assume PPQ 96 (offbeat tick = 96·r/(r+1)); MPC swing % = r/(r+1)×100.

| BPM | ratio (derived) | offbeat position / MPC % | FL tick @PPQ96 |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≤120 (slow/ballad) | ~3:1–3.5:1 (sourced cap) | 75–78% | 72–75 |
| 150 | 3:1 | 75% | 72 |
| 180 | 2.3:1 | 70% | 67 |
| 200 | 2:1 | 66.7% (triplet) | 64 |
| 240 | 1.5:1 | 60% | 58 |
| 300+ | ~1:1 | 50–55% | 48–53 |

Caution: these are ride-cymbal ratios for drummers; see Gaps re soloists/bass.

#### Core swing grids (12-step triplet grid, one 4/4 bar; convention)
```
Ride     x . . | X . x | x . . | X . x     ("ding, ding-da, ding, ding-da"; accent 2 & 4)
HH foot  . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .     (chick on 2 & 4)
Kick     g . . | g . . | g . . | g . .     (feathered, vel 20–35) + occasional X bomb on 12 or 3
Snare    . . g | . . . | . . x | . . .     (comping; vary each bar, often on the "let" = 3rd triplet)
Bass     X . . | x . . | x . . | x . .     (walking quarters)
Piano    . . . | . . x | . . . | . . .     (Charleston: beat 1 + "and of 2" = steps 1 & 6, or just offbeats)
```
16-step equivalent: `Ride x . . . | X . x . | x . . . | X . x .` — but the offbeats at steps 7 and 15 must be delayed toward the triplet position (two-thirds of the way from step 7 to step 8 for 2:1). **FL Studio caution (convention):** the channel-rack swing knob delays the even 16th steps (2, 4, 6…), which swings 16ths, not 8ths — for jazz 8th-note swing, program in the piano roll with snap = 1/3 beat (triplet) and nudge per the table, or use a 12-step pattern grid.

#### Shuffle (blues) — 12-step (convention)
```
HH/ride  x . x | x . x | x . x | x . x     (all triplet "long-short" 8ths)
Snare    . . . | X . . | . . . | X . .
Kick     X . . | x . . | X . . | x . .
Guitar   X . x | X . x | X . x | X . x     (boogie: root-5 / root-6 / root-b7 / root-6 shapes)
```
Shuffle = both 8ths of every beat played (long-short); swing ride = skip note only on 2 & 4.

#### Walking bass rules (convention)
- Beat 1 = chord root (or 3rd); beats 2–3 = chord/scale tones; beat 4 = chromatic approach (half step above/below next root) or the next chord's 5th.
- Two-feel (half notes on 1 & 3) in heads/first chorus, then walk in 4 for solos (classic build).
- Register (upright, sounding): ~E1 (MIDI 28) to G3 (MIDI 55); FL Studio names middle C (MIDI 60) "C5", so this is E2–G4 in FL's labels.
- Example over Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 (one bar each): `D F A Ab | G B D Db | C E G B` (Ab and Db are half-step-above approaches to the next root) (derived from rules).

#### Comping (convention)
- Rhythms: Charleston (1, &2), anticipations on "and of 4" into next bar, Freddie Green four-to-bar (swing era), short staccato stabs; leave space when soloist is busy.
- Voicings: shells and rootless voicings (see Q3); avoid doubling the bass's root.

### Inferences
- Rule of thumb for the composer-AI: ride swing = tempo-dependent (table); bass and comping slightly straighter than the ride; heads/horns laid back a few ms (inference from general practice — see Gaps).
- Applying the 100 ms rule makes 2:1 appear right around 200 BPM, consistent with "2:1 only at a certain tempo" (derived).

### Gaps
- Soloist-vs-drummer microtiming (soloists delaying downbeats and syncing offbeats; lower soloist swing ratios) is widely cited from Friberg & Sundström 2002 but was not confirmed on the fetched lay page.
- No per-tempo numeric table from the original paper was retrieved (only endpoints and the 100 ms rule); Honing & Haas 2008 (tempo-invariance of swing) found but not read.

## 3. How are jazz ensembles voiced and arranged (head–solos–head, trading fours, shout chorus, sections, shell voicings)?

### Takeaway
Small-group jazz follows intro → head → solo choruses → trading fours → head → tag; big bands add section backgrounds, a shout chorus and soli. Voicings prioritise guide tones (3 & 7): shells (1-3-7 / 1-7-3) for guitar/left hand, rootless 4-note voicings for piano, quartal for modal, close/drop-2 for horn sections.

### Cited Findings
- Shell voicings contain only 1, 3 and 7 (5th omitted); pioneered on guitar by Freddie Green and Django Reinhardt; staple of traditional jazz guitar — [Jazz Guitar Lessons: shell voicings](https://learn.jazzguitarlessons.net/blog/shell-voicings-jazz-guitar); [The Jazz Resource](https://www.thejazzresource.com/shell_voicings.html).
- Guide tones (3rd, 7th) move chromatically/stepwise between chords and determine chord quality — [Bass Musician Magazine](https://bassmusicianmagazine.com/2012/08/guide-tones-by-joshua-barnhart/).
- Big-band sections: saxophones (reeds), trumpets, trombones, rhythm; call-and-response between brass and reeds; riff-based compositions with improvised solos — [Wikipedia: Swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music).
- McCoy Tyner's quartal voicings (chords in fourths) give motion without leaving the mode — [Wikipedia: Modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz).
- New Orleans: polyphonic collective improvisation, trumpet leads melody — [Wikipedia: Dixieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixieland).

#### Voicing cookbook in C (convention / derived)
- Shells ii–V–I: `Dm7 = D-C-F (1-7-3)` → `G7 = G-F-B (1-7-3)`... alternate 1-3-7 / 1-7-3 for smooth motion: `Dm7 D-F-C`, `G7 G-F-B`, `Cmaj7 C-E-B`.
- Rootless (piano LH, bass plays root), A/B forms: `Dm9 = F-A-C-E` → `G13 = F-A-B-E` → `Cmaj9 = E-G-B-D`; only 1–2 notes move per chord (derived from 3-5-7-9 / 7-9-3-13 formulas).
- Altered dominant: `G7alt rootless = F-Ab-B-Eb` (b7, b9, 3, #5) (derived).
- Quartal (modal, D Dorian): `E-A-D-G-B` ("So What" chord), shift it diatonically up/down a step.
- Horn sections: 4-way close (melody on top, chord tones below within an octave), drop-2 (2nd voice from top dropped an octave) for saxes; trumpets in 4-part close up high; trombones in 4ths/shells below; bari doubles bass line or root (convention).

#### Arrangement templates (convention)
- Small group (5–7 min): intro 4–8 bars (vamp or last 4 of tune) → head 1–2x → solos 2–4 choruses each (horn, then piano, then bass) → trading fours (horns/drums alternate 4 bars) or eights → head out → tag ending (repeat last 4 bars 2–3x) or ritard/fermata.
- Big band (3–5 min): intro → melody (saxes or brass lead, other sections answer) → solo with section background pads/riffs → soli (one section harmonised, e.g. sax soli) → shout chorus (full band, loudest, trumpets high, maximum density, usually second-to-last chorus) → recap/coda with final "button" hit.
- Blues band: intro turnaround → vocal choruses with horn/harp fills between lines → solo choruses → stop-time chorus → vocal → turnaround ending (I–IV–I–V7 then I9 final).

### Inferences
- For MIDI generation, a "chorus" is the atomic unit (12 or 32 bars); arrangements are built by chorus count, with energy increasing via bass two-feel → four-feel, drums brushes → sticks, and backgrounds entering in later solo choruses.

### Gaps
- No primary source fetched on shout chorus/soli conventions or standard big-band instrumentation counts (17-piece figure is convention).

## 4. How do producers make realistic jazz/blues in a DAW?

### Takeaway
Realism comes from (a) tempo-correct swing on the right subdivision, (b) wide, role-specific velocity ranges, (c) small timing offsets and real-time-played cymbals, (d) idiomatic parts (walking bass, rootless comping, brushes), and (e) realistic acoustic sample libraries with articulations, mixed with room ambience and modest compression.

### Cited Findings
- Small timing offsets ahead/behind the beat create "push and pull"; uniform velocity lacks a drummer's dynamic range; jazz patterns need extra attention to dynamics — [Mastering.com: realistic MIDI drums](https://mastering.com/?p=14279); [Mixelite: humanizing MIDI drums](https://mixelite.com/blog/humanizing-midi-drums/).
- Program in real time with velocity-sensitive controllers, especially cymbals; randomise velocity ±X% (more useful on slow drums) and timing (more on fast) — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/effective-drum-programming-part-2).
- Feathered kick must be barely audible — [Cruise Ship Drummer](https://cruiseshipdrummer.com/?p=796).

#### Velocity bands (MIDI 1–127; convention)
- Ride: beats 2 & 4 = 90–105, beats 1 & 3 = 75–90, skip notes = 55–75; random ±5.
- Hi-hat foot (2 & 4) = 60–80. Feathered kick = 20–35; bombs = 95–115.
- Snare comping ghosts = 25–45; accents/fills = 85–110. Brush sweep = continuous sample, taps 40–70.
- Walking bass = 70–95, beat 1 slightly higher, approach notes softer; occasional ghosted "skip" triplet.
- Piano comping = 50–80; melody/solo = 70–110 with phrase-shaped crescendo/decrescendo.

#### Timing (convention)
- Ride/bass: on the grid ±5 ms, bass may sit slightly ahead ("on top") at fast tempos; horns/solo lines 5–20 ms behind on downbeats for "laid back"; never apply identical random offsets to all parts.
- Swing the 8th-note subdivision in jazz (triplet piano-roll snap + nudge per Q2 table), the 16th in jazz-hop/neo-soul.

#### Sounds (convention; FL stock in brackets)
- Upright bass: multisampled upright library with finger noise/slides (e.g. NI Session Bassist Upright, or Kontakt/third-party); avoid synth bass for acoustic styles. [No convincing FL stock upright — use DirectWave/Slicex with a sampled upright pack.]
- Drums: jazz kit libraries with brushes (sweeps + taps) and ride with long sustain [FPC with a jazz kit sample pack]; keep kick 18–20" tuned high.
- Keys: acoustic grand; Rhodes/Wurli with tremolo/chorus (Arturia Stage-73, Spectrasonics Keyscape) [FL Keys]; Hammond with Leslie for soul jazz/acid jazz.
- Horns: solo sax/trumpet/trombone libraries with breath-controller (CC2/CC11) dynamics, scoops, falls, growls; layering several solo instruments reads more real than "section" patches for small groups.
- Guitar: amp-sim for Chicago blues; nylon-string for bossa; archtop clean (neck pickup, tone rolled off) for jazz.

#### Mix character (convention)
- Classic acoustic jazz: natural room/plate reverb (Fruity Convolver with room IR), light bus compression (1–3 dB), wide dynamic range; masters typically well below pop loudness (≈ −14 to −18 LUFS-I).
- 1950s Blue Note-style: close-miked horns, bright ride, short room; vintage = mono-ish or hard-panned early stereo.
- Blues: amp grit, slapback delay (80–120 ms), mono-centred vocal; delta = narrow-band 78 sound.
- Smooth/fusion/acid: modern pop polish, chorus/reverb, compressed bass and drums.

#### FL Studio workflow (convention)
1. Set tempo, choose subdivision (swung 8ths vs straight 16ths) by genre card; set piano-roll snap to 1/3 beat for swing styles.
2. Drums: ride + hi-hat foot + feathered kick first; then write snare comping varying every bar; apply velocity bands and ±5–10 ms random timing (Piano roll → Tools → Randomize, Alt+R).
3. Walking bass with approach tones per chord; two-feel in the head.
4. Piano/guitar comping with shells/rootless voicings on syncopated, sparse rhythms.
5. Horn head in unison/harmony; solos built from chord-tone targets on beats with bebop-scale passing tones.
6. Mix with room reverb on a send, minimal compression, pan like a bandstand (drums center-wide, bass center, piano slightly left, horns slightly right).

#### Most common "fake" tells (convention)
- Fixed 2:1 swing at every tempo, or swinging 16ths via the channel-rack swing knob instead of 8ths.
- Machine-gun velocities; ride with no 2&4 accents; loud kick on every beat.
- Bass playing only roots/arpeggios with no chromatic approaches; piano playing full root-position chords on every beat (clashing with bass).
- Sustained horns with no attack/breath/vibrato automation; quantized "perfect" section hits.
- Blue notes as fixed b3/b5 instead of bends; swinging bossa/ragtime; huge hall reverb.

### Inferences
- The biggest realism lever is the ride cymbal (velocity + tempo-correct swing), followed by a walking bass with chromatic approaches; get these right before improving samples.

### Gaps
- No sourced comparison of specific jazz sample libraries (upright bass, brush kits) was retrieved; product names are examples only.
- No sourced loudness (LUFS) data for jazz masters; the range given is convention.

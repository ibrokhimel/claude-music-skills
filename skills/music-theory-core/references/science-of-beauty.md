## 1. Consonance is learned harmonicity

- **Plomp & Levelt (1965)**: two pure tones sound rough when closer than a critical band (roughness peaks near 1/4 critical band); the critical band spans a wider musical interval at low frequencies, and Bach/Dvořák voicings thin out in the bass accordingly **(sourced)**.
  Do: keep 2nds and 3rds out of the low register; voice the bass in octaves, 5ths and 10ths; put clusters above ~MIDI 60.
- **McDermott, Lehr & Oxenham (2010)**, >250 listeners: liking for consonant chords tracked preference for *harmonic* spectra, not dislike of beating; it grew with years of instrument playing **(sourced)**.
  Do: for "sweet/stable", choose intervals whose partials line up (octave, 5th, 4th, root-position major, sus); for "tense", add m2/TT/clusters or inharmonic timbres (bells, FM, metallic).
- **McDermott et al. (2016)**, Tsimane' of Bolivia: with minimal Western exposure they rated consonant and dissonant chords equally pleasant; preference strengthened with Western exposure **(sourced)**.
  Do: treat "consonant = pleasant" as a style setting for Western-pop listeners, not physics. Genres can and do live on dissonance (drill b2, horror clusters).
- **Bowling, Purves & Gill (2018)**: consonance of all chromatic dyads, triads and tetrads was predicted by similarity to the harmonic spectra of voiced speech **(sourced)**.
  Do: when a part must feel warm and human, use harmonically aligned voicings and voice-like timbres (EP, pads, choirs); bells and FM read as colder.
- Timbre × harmony **(inference)**: a consonant chord on a distorted or inharmonic patch can still sound rough.
  Do: on distorted leads and saturated 808s, simplify the harmony (power chords, single lines, octaves).

## 2. Expectation: surprise in certainty, certainty after uncertainty

- **Huron, ITPRA (Sweet Anticipation, 2006)**: Imagination, Tension, Prediction, Reaction, Appraisal. Correct predictions are rewarded; surprises trigger a fast negative reaction that slower appraisal can flip into pleasure ("contrastive valence") **(sourced, via review)**.
  Do: make the listener able to predict (clear meter, repeated pattern, functional harmony) before violating anything; use dissonance as set-up (suspend 4–3, 9–8, 7–6, then resolve on a strong beat), not as an end state.
- **Cheung et al. (2019, Current Biology)**: ~80,000 chords from Billboard songs modelled for uncertainty and surprise. Pleasure peaked for **low uncertainty + high surprise** and for **high uncertainty + low surprise**; amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex tracked the interaction **(sourced)**.
  Do: after three loops of a predictable progression, spend one unexpected chord (bVII, iv, bVI); after an ambiguous or wandering passage, land on the most expected chord (home tonic). Never stack surprise on surprise.
- **Gold et al. (2019, J. Neurosci.)**: intermediate-complexity fragments were liked most (inverted U); repetition lowered liking but kept the preference shape **(sourced)**.
  Do: if a section feels boring, add novelty in ONE dimension (rhythm, harmony or timbre); if it feels chaotic, add repetition.
- **Matthews et al. (2019, PLOS ONE)**: groove followed an inverted U with rhythmic complexity, and rhythm worked best with **medium-complexity chords**; harmony mainly drove the pleasure component **(sourced)**.
  Do: busy syncopated drums want 7th-chord-level harmony, not dense altered jazz chords; plain triads are fine when the drums are simple.
- Genre baselines **(inference)**: surprise is relative to the listener's genre model — bVII is mildly surprising in pop and a cliché in rock.
  Do: measure "surprise" against the genre card's normal vocabulary, not against textbook theory.

## 3. Reward: anticipation and payoff (builds and drops)

- **Salimpoor et al. (2011, Nature Neuroscience)**, PET: dopamine released in the striatum at peak emotion; caudate tied to anticipation, nucleus accumbens to the peak **(sourced)**.
  Do: design two reward phases — a clearly signalled lead-in (riser, filter sweep, fill, pre-chorus lift) and a payoff that delivers.
- Why drops work **(inference: Salimpoor + Huron + Cheung)**: the build raises certainty that a big event is coming; the drop delivers it with a big contrast in loudness, bass and texture; a short silence or fake-out is a small surprise inside a high-certainty context — Cheung's high-pleasure zone.
  Do: make risers predictable in direction; make the payoff add something new (new instrument, register or harmonic twist), not just volume; clear kick and bass 4–16 bars before the drop so their return is felt.

## 4. Chills and tears

- **Sloboda (1991)**, 83 listeners, 38 passages: tears were linked most reliably to melodic **appoggiaturas** and **sequences** **(sourced)**; shivers to new or unprepared harmonies and sudden dynamic/texture change **[unverified]**. Only "hit" passages were analysed.
  Do: for a tear-jerk moment, put a dissonant non-chord tone on a strong beat that resolves by step, inside a melodic sequence that climbs.
- **Grewe, Kopiez & Altenmüller (2007)**: chills mark individual emotional peaks, aligned with physiological peaks **(sourced)**; triggers include a new voice or section entering **[unverified]**.
  Do: mark peaks with a new voice/instrument entering, not just more level.
- **Nagel et al. (2008)**, same group: chill excerpts showed loudness increases specifically in **~920–4400 Hz (8–18 Bark)** **(sourced)**.
  Do: at climaxes raise presence-band energy (upper harmonies, open filters, lead/vocal forward, string or synth brightness), and leave headroom there in earlier sections.
- **de Fleurian & Pearce (2021)**, >700 chill songs vs same-artist controls: chill songs were sadder, slower, less intense and more instrumental **(sourced)**.
  Do: chills come from contrast and exposure, not bombast — a sparse, slow passage with an exposed melody before a swell is a reliable vehicle.

## 5. Groove: medium syncopation, dense subdivisions, tight timing

- **Janata, Tomic & Haberman (2012)**: groove = urge to move + positive affect; listeners agree on it; sensorimotor coupling predicts it **(sourced)**.
  Do: prioritise a clear, danceable pulse before decoration.
- **Witek et al. (2014, PLOS ONE)**, 50 funk breaks at 120 BPM: **medium syncopation** gave the most desire to move and pleasure (inverted U; R² .347 move, .427 pleasure) **(sourced)**. The body "fills in" the displaced beat.
  Do: keep anchors (kick on 1, backbeat on 2&4 or 3) and put syncopation in hats, ghosts, bass and secondary kicks (e.g. kick on the "&" of 2 or 3). Never syncopate every layer.
- **Senn et al. (2018)**, 248 patterns, 665 listeners, 8,329 ratings: syncopation and **event density** raised groove; beat salience, residual microtiming and variability had no effect; familiarity raised groove **(sourced)**.
  Do: use a dense subdivision layer (16th hats/shakers) and genre-standard templates; don't rely on random humanisation.
- **Davies et al. (2013)**: fully quantised versions earned the highest groove ratings; systematic microtiming lowered them except the simple jazz shuffle. **Senn et al. (2016)**: a "high-groove zone" from quantised to original timing; exaggerated microtiming hurt. **Kilchenmann & Senn (2015)**: experts moved more to tight (60% reduced) than fully quantised timing **(sourced; contested area)**.
  Do: quantise by default; apply swing as a consistent ratio; keep humanisation within ±10–20 ms and systematic (late snare = laid back, early hats = pushing); vary velocity more than timing.

## 6. Low frequencies carry the beat

- **Lenc et al. (2018, PNAS)**, EEG: rhythms in low tones (130 Hz) gave stronger neural tracking at the beat frequency than the same rhythms in high tones (1237 Hz), especially when syncopated **(sourced)**.
  Do: put the core pulse in kick and bass; when the pattern is syncopated, let the bass/808 help define the beat.
- **Hove et al. (2014, PNAS)**: timing deviations were detected better in the lower of two streams and tapping followed it (cochlear origin); pitch is encoded better in the higher stream; across cultures rhythm sits low and melody high **(sourced)**.
  Do: timing precision matters most in kick and bass — lock bass notes to kick hits or to clearly defined gaps; put the tune in the highest prominent voice.

## 7. Preferred tempo (~2 Hz)

- **Moelants (2002)**, >70,000 DJ-list tracks plus tapping: tempos cluster at **115–127 BPM, peak near 125**; preferred range ~67–150 BPM **(sourced via secondary paper)**.
  Do: for maximum danceability sit near 115–130 BPM; slower genres (hip hop 85–95) keep 16th or 8th hats so a ~2 Hz layer is present; faster ones (140 trap, 174 DnB) use half-time snares so the body can lock to 70/87 BPM.

## 8. Repetition, familiarity and earworms

- **Margulis (On Repeat, 2014)**: repetition is much of what makes sound register as music; Huron estimates >90% of what people hear is music they've heard before **(sourced)**.
  Do: repeat motifs, riffs and hooks deliberately; repetition is not lazy.
- **Deutsch, Henthorn & Lapidis (2011)**, speech-to-song illusion: a spoken phrase repeated ~10 times starts to sound sung; transposing or jumbling the repeats kills it **(sourced)**.
  Do: repeat a vocal chop or spoken tag at the **identical** pitch to make it melodic.
- **Gold et al. (2019)**: identical repetition lowers liking **(sourced)**.
  Do: keep the hook constant but change its surroundings on each return (drums, bass, harmony underneath, register, added layer). Repeat 2- or 4-bar units 2–4 times, then vary **(inference)**.
- **Jakubowski et al. (2017)**, 100 earworms vs 100 matched tunes: earworms had **faster tempi**, **common global contours** (rise-then-fall arch) and **less common interval patterns** between turning points; popularity also predicted earworm status **(sourced)**.
  Do: build hooks on a familiar contour with one distinctive leap or rhythmic quirk; moderate-to-fast tempos help stickiness.
- **Senn et al. (2018)**: familiar drum patterns groove more **(sourced)**.
  Do: innovate on top of a recognisable genre template rather than abandoning it.

## 9. Melody perception

- **von Hippel & Huron (2000)**: post-skip reversal and gap-fill are largely regression to the mean — leaps land near the edge of the range, so the line must turn back **(sourced)**.
  Do: after a leap of a 5th or more away from the centre, return toward the middle, usually by step. Mostly steps, one climax per phrase.
- **Bregman, Auditory Scene Analysis (1990)** **[unverified]**: sounds fuse into one stream when close in pitch, similar in timbre and onset; distant fast alternations split.
  Do: separate parts by register, timbre, onset timing and pan; layer sounds that should fuse with identical onsets and octave-related pitches.

## 10. Emotion cues

- **Fritz et al. (2009)**: Mafa listeners in Cameroon, unfamiliar with Western music, recognised happy, sad and fearful pieces above chance **(sourced)**.
  Do: use tempo, loudness, register and articulation as the primary emotion signal; mode is a secondary, Western convention.
- **Juslin, BRECVEMA (2013)**: brain-stem reflex, rhythmic entrainment, evaluative conditioning, contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory, musical expectancy, aesthetic judgement **(sourced)**.
  Do: pick the lever per section — a sudden hit (reflex), a steady pulse (entrainment), a crying-voice-like falling melody (contagion), a nostalgic palette such as vinyl or 80s synths (memory), expectancy play (section 2), virtuosic craft (aesthetic awe).
- **Sachs, Damasio & Habibi (2015)**: sad music pleases because the sadness is aesthetically distanced and safe **(sourced)**.
  Do: frame sadness with beauty — minor ballad, consonant voicings, lush reverb, a resolving cadence.
- Cue map (happy/sad/tension/anger) is in `genre-cards.md` §5 **(convention, Juslin & Laukka 2003 [unverified])**.

## 11. Loudness and dynamic contrast

- **Vickers (2010/2011, AES)**: loudness does not correlate with sales, has little effect on preference between songs, and hypercompression side effects are disliked **(sourced)**. Evidence on compression itself is mixed (Aalborg: no effect; Goldsmiths 2014: heavier compression lowered quality ratings) **(sourced)**.
  Do: master to platform norms instead of maximal loudness; keep verse-to-chorus and pre-drop-to-drop level and density contrast; compare mix decisions loudness-matched. Targets and chains: `music-mix-master`.

## 12. From findings to budgets

| Budget | Rule | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Attention** | 3–4 focal elements at once (foreground plane); exactly one sub owner below ~100 Hz; every new part gets a role and plane before notes | Owsinski arrangement elements; "rule of three"; Plomp & Levelt masking; Hove pitch-high/timing-low **(convention + sourced mechanism)** |
| **Surprise** | one surprise per predictable context (4–8 bars of established pattern); land expected after ambiguity; never surprise on surprise; save the biggest twist for the last hook/drop | Cheung 2019; Huron; Jakubowski 2017; Sloboda 1991 **(sourced direction, convention amounts)** |
| **Complexity** | medium syncopation × medium harmony; raise one dimension, lower another; dense subdivision + clear anchors | Witek 2014; Matthews 2019; Gold 2019; Senn 2018 **(sourced direction)** |

Corollaries: hold the progression steady when the drums get busy; save the borrowed chord for the fourth loop; clear the low end and presence band before a drop so the payoff has headroom; vary the second drop/hook.

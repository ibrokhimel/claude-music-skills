## 1. Earworm research

Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart & Müllensiefen, "Dissecting an Earworm", *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* (online Nov 2016, 2017 volume) **(sourced)**:
- About 3,000 participants named their frequent earworms. These were compared with tunes never named as earworms but matched for UK chart popularity and recency. Genres were pop, rock, rap and R&B, from data collected in 2010–2013. Group sizes of about 100 each are reported in summaries but were not verified in-session.
- Earworms had:
  1. **faster tempi**;
  2. **more common global contours**, such as the rise-and-fall of "Twinkle Twinkle", which "Moves Like Jagger" also follows;
  3. **less common interval patterns between turning points**: unexpected leaps, or more repeated notes than usual (e.g. "My Sharona", "In the Mood", "Smoke on the Water").
- Chart success and recency also predicted earworm status.
- The most-named earworms were "Bad Romance", "Can't Get You Out of My Head", "Don't Stop Believin'", "Somebody That I Used to Know" and "Moves Like Jagger".

Translation for a generator **(derived)**: **familiar shape + one locally unusual feature + lively note rate**. Use an arch or a descent to 1, 3 or 5. Then spend exactly one surprise inside it: a leap of a 6th, 7th or octave, a run of repeated notes broken by a leap, or a borrowed note. At slow BPMs, raise the note rate with 8ths or 16ths.

## 2. Surprise, complexity and repetition budgets

- **Cheung et al. 2019** modelled about 80,000 Billboard chords. Pleasure peaked at **low uncertainty + high surprise**, or **high uncertainty + low surprise** **(sourced)**. For melody this means: after a predictable stretch (repeats, sequence), spend one surprise; after an ambiguous stretch, land on the most expected note (root or 3rd on the downbeat) **(derived)**.
- **Gold et al. 2019**: liking follows an inverted U over complexity. Repetition lowers liking but does not remove the preference for intermediate complexity **(sourced)**. Do not stack surprise on surprise. If the rhythm is busy (16ths, syncopation), keep the pitch set small, and the reverse **(derived)**.
- **Huron's ITPRA**: a surprise triggers a fast negative reaction that slower appraisal can reframe as pleasant, which he calls "contrastive valence" **(sourced)**. The appraisal step is why a single surprise inside a familiar frame works, and why a melody made entirely of surprises reads as random.
- **Repetition:**
  - Over 90% of music people hear is music they have heard before (Huron, cited by Margulis).
  - Exact-pitch repetition turns speech into song (Deutsch).
  - Identical repetition lowers liking (Gold) **(sourced)**.
  - Rule: keep the hook constant and vary its surroundings on each return (drums, harmony, register, layers) **(derived)**.
- Budget details and the wider science (consonance, groove, dopamine) live in `music-theory-core`.

## 3. Chills: where a melody moves people

- **Sloboda 1991** (*Psychology of Music* 19:110–120): 83 listeners identified passages that gave them physical reactions. Tears were linked most reliably to **melodic appoggiaturas and melodic sequences**. Caveat: only passages that worked were analysed, so the failure rate of these devices is unknown **(sourced)**.
- **Grewe et al. 2007**: chills mark individual emotional peaks and line up with physiological peaks. They tend to occur at structural events such as a new voice or section entering, or a loudness increase. Responses are highly individual **(sourced)**.
- **Nagel et al. 2008**: chill excerpts showed a loudness increase in the **920–4400 Hz (8–18 Bark)** band **(sourced)**. Put the climax note where it adds presence-range energy: a higher register, an open filter, an added upper octave. Do not rely on sub energy for it.
- **Salimpoor et al. 2011**: dopamine is released both in anticipation (caudate) and at the peak (nucleus accumbens) **(sourced)**. A pre-chorus that audibly climbs toward a predictable arrival maximises the payoff.
- **de Fleurian & Pearce 2021**: across 700+ chill-inducing songs, chill songs were on average sadder, slower, less intense and more instrumental than matched tracks by the same artists **(sourced)**. An exposed, slow melody with an appoggiatura over a sparse bed is a reliable vehicle.

Practical "chill recipe" **(derived)**: build predictability (sequence the motif twice), then put an accented appoggiatura or suspension at the climax (55–75% through, strong beat, highest note), resolve it by step, and enter a new layer or register at that same moment. Ex 2 (pop) and Ex 9 (cinematic) in `worked-examples.md` follow it.

## 4. Hook checklist and scoring

Score candidate motifs and keep the best of 3–5 **(convention, built on the findings above)**:

| # | Criterion | Pass condition | Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Common contour | arch, or descent ending on 1/3/5 | 2 |
| 2 | Compact | motif 2–4 beats, 3–7 distinct pitches, span ≤ 9 semitones | 1 |
| 3 | Repetition | repeats ≥ 2× within the hook; the hook recurs ≥ 4–8× per song | 2 |
| 4 | Exactly one surprise | one of: a 6th/7th/octave leap, a borrowed/chromatic note, a displacement, a repeated-note run broken by a leap, an appoggiatura | 2 (0 if none, 0 if 3+) |
| 5 | Lively rate | 8ths or 16ths at slow BPM; ≥ 4 onsets per bar | 1 |
| 6 | Singable | 65–80% steps; no augmented intervals unless genre-idiomatic | 1 |
| 7 | Rhythm identity | at least one syncopation and one rest; rhythm survives being clapped | 1 |
| 8 | Climax placement | highest note once, at 55–75%, on a strong beat (title or main word here) | 1 |
| 9 | Implies harmony | soloed, the chord changes are audible | 1 |

Keep a hook at 9 or more out of 12. At 6 or below, rewrite the rhythm first, then the pitches.

## 5. Producer craft

**(sourced, practitioner)**
- Simple ideas are easier to process, recall and sing. Many iconic EDM melodies use surprisingly few notes (Lost Stories Academy).
- Refine hooks by **subtraction**: change one note, shift a rhythm, remove a phrase. Removing notes often helps more than adding them (Lost Stories Academy).
- Every memorable drop has **one element that becomes its identity** (Lost Stories Academy).
- Use **only one lead sound at a time** in a drop, so listeners have one thing to latch onto (Bax-shop).
- Write a strong, singable, not-too-busy lead over a simple 4-chord loop (Bax-shop).
- Keep trap melodies simple and repetitive, because the 808 and hats are busy (RouteNote).

Placement **(convention)**: tease the hook in the first 5–15 seconds. In modern pop, the first chorus arrives by about 30–45 seconds, and recent Hot 100 #1s average about 38 seconds per a hit-song analysis (search-level source). Restate the hook in the chorus or drop and again in the post-chorus or outro.

## 6. Genre melody style cards

Format: **scale · register · rhythm/density · devices · sound**. The genre cards are mostly **(convention)**; the cited items are marked. BPM, drums and bass for each genre: `music-theory-core` genre cards, `music-drums-groove`, `music-bass-808`.

**Trap (130–160, half-time).** Natural minor with harmonic-minor V, or Phrygian **(sourced: PML)** · bells 64–84, plus an optional −12 layer kept above 52 · 4–8 onsets per bar with lots of space and tresillo cells · b6→5 sighs, the leading tone over V, octave doubling, a 2- or 4-bar loop, simple and repetitive so the 808 has room **(sourced: RouteNote)** · bells, plucks, dark piano, flute, strings, choir. Counter-melody from the root, octave, 5th and m7 **(sourced: NI)**. Worked: Ex 1.

**UK drill (138–151).** Dark minor with b2 colour and harmonic minor **(sourced: NI/Wikipedia)** · high register (72–88) against low piano or strings · 2–4-note **cold motifs**; leave a gap every bar; triplet-feel 8ths and quarter triplets · b2 and b6 neighbours, sliding 808 carrying melodic weight **(sourced)** · piano, music-box bells, strings, choir pads. Worked: Ex 8.

**NY/Brooklyn drill.** Same skeleton with simpler minor triads, hook-forward repetition and heavier 808 glides **(sourced: Orphiq/Beatstorapon)**. Melodies are more direct and often sample-based (vocal or R&B flips). Keep the motif on 1–2 pitches with a strong rhythm.

**Plugg / pluggnb (130–150 half-time).** Major-ish dreamy or minor; pluggnb uses electric-piano block chords with m7/add9 **(sourced: Reason/Splice)** · lead 67–88 · sparse sine or square leads, glassy bells, short repeating phrases · target 9ths and maj7s, end on colour tones · sine lead, glassy bell, Rhodes.

**Rage (140–170).** Minor · wide, high synth hook 69–91 · **short looping stereo-wide future-bass synth lead** with a game-music feel **(sourced: Wikipedia)**; 1-bar or 2-bar arp-like riffs in 8ths or 16ths · repetition over development; distort and stack octaves · supersaw, square arps.

**Phonk.** Minor or Phrygian · cowbell melodies 72–96 · **TR-808 cowbell riffs, often tresillo-based** **(sourced: Wikipedia)** · b2, chromatic neighbours, heavy repetition · distorted cowbell, Memphis vocal chops.

**Boom bap (85–95).** The sample's key, minor or Dorian jazz-soul · melody often lives in the sample; add a sparse 2–4-note horn or keys stab answer · swing 8ths/16ths **(MPC 57% kick/snare, 74% hats, sourced: Attack)** · call-and-response with the sample's phrase · horns, Rhodes, vibes.

**Lo-fi (60–90).** Dorian or minor pentatonic over maj7/m7/9 chords · keys 57–79 · 3–6 onsets per bar, lazy swung 8ths (55–62%), rests · target 7ths and 9ths, end on the 9th or maj7 (unresolved), humanize ±10–20 ms · Rhodes, nylon guitar, muted trumpet, vinyl texture. Worked: Ex 4.

**R&B / neo-soul (60–90, or 120–140 half-time).** Major-7 or minor-9 colour; runs from the key's pentatonic **(sourced: MusicGurus)**, adding b3/b7 blue notes for grit · topline 57–79 · syllabic body; **runs and melismas usually descend at phrase ends** **(sourced: DeviantNoise)**; 16th-note triplet runs; pushes everywhere · target 7ths, 9ths and 13ths; guide-tone motion; riffs are short answers, runs are featured phrases **(sourced)** · voice, Rhodes, pitched vocal chops. Worked: Ex 5.

**Pop topline (95–125 dance, 70–90 ballad).**
- Verse: speech-like, narrow (5–9 semitones), many repeated notes.
- Pre-chorus: rises (ascending sequence) and may use an odd length.
- Chorus: 3–5 semitones higher, longer notes, title on the climax or downbeat; often a 2-bar call plus a 2-bar response.
- Range ≤ 14 semitones within the singer's window.
- Hook rhythm matters more than harmony **(convention)**.
- Worked: Ex 2 (+ Ex 3 counter).

**EDM drop / progressive / big room (124–128).** Minor or major over vi–IV–I–V or i–VI–III–VII · lead 64–84, stacked ±12 · a 1- or 2-bar riff of 4–8 notes, repeated 4× with a changed 4th ending (A A A A′); 16th or syncopated-8th rhythm; mostly chord tones · one lead at a time **(sourced: Bax-shop)**, call-and-response with a vocal chop or octave · supersaw (seven detuned saws, **sourced: Orphiq**), pluck. Worked: Ex 6.

**Future bass (130–175, half-time).** Major-leaning with maj7/m7/add9 **(sourced: Violet Recording)** · lead is vocal chops plus a pitch-glide supersaw · chords play short rhythmic 16ths with bend swells, and the lead answers in gaps · use portamento between chop pitches.

**Trance (132–140).** Natural minor (i–VI–III–VII), sus2/sus4 held before resolving · anthem lead 69–93 · 16th gated plucks and arps under a long-held breakdown theme · beatless breakdown exposes the theme **(sourced: Orphiq)**; climax on a held top note; optional +1/+2 semitone key lift for the last chorus · supersaw, pluck, arp.

**House (classic/deep, 118–126).** Minor or Dorian vamps, gospel major for vocal house · melodic content lives in the **top voice of off-beat chord stabs** (steps 3 7 11 15, or 3-3-2), which moves by step; m7/m9 stab voicings · piano stabs (M1-style), organ, Rhodes, diva vocal. Deep house avoids big builds and favours hypnotic repetition **(sourced: Wikipedia)**.

**Tech house (124–128).** Often no chords · 1–2-note stabs, plucked percussive synths, **vocal one-shot hooks** **(sourced: Wikipedia, snippet-level)** · stationary contour; the rhythm is the hook.

**Afro house (118–124).** Dorian or Aeolian modal vamps · marimba or kalimba-like plucks, flute, chants; **long evolving 8–16-bar melodies**; 3-3-2 figures · call-and-response vocals **(sourced: Wikipedia)**.

**Amapiano (108–115).** m9/maj7/6-9 jazz harmony · piano lead 60–84, smooth, over minor-7th and 9th chords; arpeggiators for flow **(sourced: Beats To Rap On / SampleFocus)** · common tones between chords give a hypnotic line; call-and-response with the **log drum**, which carries the bass melody and must stay tuned **(sourced)**. Keep the lead above about C4 so the log drum owns the low register. Elements enter gradually.

**Afrobeats (95–115).** Major pentatonic or major-mode toplines · lead 64–84 · lots of repeated notes, 3-3-2 syncopation, pushes, 1-bar call plus 1-bar response · guitar or pluck riffs on the 3-2 clave **(sourced: Audeobox)**; a group answer widens the refrain · muted guitar, marimba, pluck, flute. Worked: Ex 7.

**Reggaeton / dembow (88–100).** Minor (i–VI–III–VII) · hooks ride the 3+3+2 dembow; short repeated-note phrases with pushes · plucks, pads, vocal hooks.

**UK garage / 2-step (124–134).** m7/m9 R&B harmony · **chopped and pitched R&B vocal hooks** **(convention)**, organ stabs; syncopated 16ths, skippy.

**Liquid DnB (172–176).** Jazz 7ths and 9ths; more harmony and melody than other DnB **(sourced: Wikipedia)** · long pad-like leads at half-time feel (one note per 1–2 beats) over 174 BPM drums · Rhodes, strings, airy vocals.

**Dubstep / riddim (140).** Minor or Phrygian, b2 and tritone menace. Melody lives in the bass's timbral movement **(sourced)** · riddim loops one mid-bass motif in triplets with silence **(sourced: BassGorilla snippet)**.

**Synthwave (80–118).** Aeolian i–VI–III–VII and i–VII–VI–VII · square or saw lead with slight portamento, 16th arps, sax or guitar solos in a solo section **(sourced, snippet-level)** · arch-shaped 8-bar themes, octave doubling.

**Hyperpop (135–170+).** Bright major, sudden modulations · brash, pitched-up, chiptune or "hypersaw" leads; earworm vocal hooks **(sourced: Wikipedia)** · extreme register (79–100) is fine.

**Cinematic / trailer.** Aeolian or Phrygian (threat); epic cadences i–VI–VII · theme 50–74 (cellos/horns), restated an octave higher · slow tempo, long notes, arch contour; an **opening leap of a 5th or 6th, then filled by step**; peak once near the end · augment the motif in later statements and reharmonize; string ostinati on a pedal tone underneath; brass and choir state the theme in Act 3 **(sourced: Violet Recording)**; "the quiet before a hit matters as much as the hit" **(sourced)**. Worked: Ex 9.

**Ambient.** Lydian or major with maj7/sus · melody optional; long tones, slow motifs over drones; texture over structure (Eno) **(sourced: Wikipedia)**.

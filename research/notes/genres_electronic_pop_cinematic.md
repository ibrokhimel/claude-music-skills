# Genre Recipes: Electronic, Pop and Cinematic (for an FL Studio track-making skill)

Scope: house (classic, deep, tech, afro), techno (Detroit, peak-time/hard), trance, drum & bass (liquid, neurofunk, vs jungle), dubstep and riddim, future bass, UK garage/2-step, synthwave/retrowave, modern pop, hyperpop, ambient/chill, cinematic/epic trailer music. Current state as of 2025–2026 plus classic foundations.

**Grid notation used throughout** (one bar of 16th notes; beats fall on steps 1, 5, 9, 13):
`X` = accent (velocity ~100–127), `x` = normal hit (~80–100), `g` = ghost (~30–60), `o` = open hat, `.` = rest. Bars are written `|1234|5678|9-12|13-16|`.
**Bars to seconds:** seconds = bars × 4 × 60 / BPM (e.g. 32 bars at 125 BPM = 61.4 s; 32 bars at 174 BPM = 44.1 s).

## Q1. What defines each genre's rhythm, sound and energy, and what distinguishes close relatives?

### Takeaway
Most of these genres are separated less by tempo than by kick placement (four-on-the-floor vs broken vs half-time), the bass's job (offbeat/rolling vs reese vs wobble vs sub), and how much energy the arrangement builds and releases. Close relatives differ in a few specific ways: deep house = slower and jazzier, built on steady hypnotic repetition; tech house = drier, groovier and more percussive. DnB = programmed two-step at about 174 BPM; jungle = chopped breakbeats with ragga/dub influence. Dubstep = half-time drops with varied sound design; riddim = one repetitive mid-bass motif with lots of space. UKG = swung garage with either a 4x4 or 2-step kick; 2-step = a kick that skips beats.

### Cited Findings

**House family**
- Trance and house share the four-on-the-floor beat; the BPM of recent festival trance styles is "on par with house music at 120 to 135" BPM. — [Wikipedia: Trance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music)
- Deep house: tempos typically 110–125 BPM, muted basslines, spacious percussion (typically TR-909), soft keyboard pads, advanced chord structures, ambient mixes, soulful vocals, and steady hypnotic repetition that *avoids* the build-up/release emphasis of other dance genres. — [Wikipedia: Deep house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_house)
- Tech house: as a mixing style it merges deep or minimal techno with the soulful, jazzy end of house plus dub elements. It feels more laid-back than progressive house, values subtlety and intricate rhythms, and avoids the "banging" side of house. — [Wikipedia: Tech house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tech_house)
- Attack Magazine's "Deep Tech House" beat: 125–130 BPM, 60–65% swing, TR-909 kick on every quarter, open hats on every offbeat, a continuous 16th shaker whose velocity variation "plays a very important role", a TR-707 clap/snare on 2 and 4 high-passed below 120 Hz, low ghost snares, and bus compression of about 4 dB. — [Attack Magazine: Deep Tech House](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/deep-tech-house/)
- Tech house beats are commonly cited at 123–126 BPM with 55–65% swing. One hat approach puts closed hats on the 1st, 2nd and 4th 16th of each beat, leaving the 3rd 16th for an open hat. — search summary of [Loopmasters tech house tutorial](https://www.loopmasters.com/articles/4300-How-To-Make-A-Tech-House-Beat-Bass-Tutorial) and Attack articles (snippet-level; not fully verified on page)
- Afro house: a South African blend of house, kwaito, mbaqanga, deep, tribal and soulful house, often sung in South African languages. BPM "commonly shifts between 80 and 122" and can reach 160. — [Wikipedia: Afro house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro_house)

**Techno family**
- Techno is generally made for continuous DJ sets at 120–150 BPM in 4/4, built on repetition. Vintage drum machines like the TR-909 (and software emulations) are prized, and rhythm is emphasised over other parameters. — [Wikipedia: Techno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)
- Detroit techno came from the Belleville Three (Juan Atkins, Kevin Saunderson, Derrick May), who favoured technological experimentation. Robert Hood (Underground Resistance) is credited with starting minimal techno as a reaction to techno becoming too "ravey" and fast. — [Wikipedia: Techno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)
- Hard techno: usually 145–160 BPM (standard techno 120–140). Distortion is a main element (kicks, bass, percussion and pads all clipped), and the sound is built around the "rumble kick", a distorted, reverb-tailed sub kick. A clean, hypnotic 145 BPM track is "fast techno, not Hard Techno"; attitude matters more than BPM. — [Futureproof Music School: What Is Hard Techno?](https://futureproofmusicschool.com/blog/what-is-hard-techno)
- Measured hard-techno tempo: median 146 BPM, interquartile range 136–155 BPM, across 9,988 tracks (measured Oct 2026). Example tracks at the median include Ultimo "303 (Sound Of The Underground)", KlangKuenstler "Weltschmerz" and Maddix "Sound of the Underground". — [Mixgraph: What BPM is Hard techno?](https://www.mixgraph.io/bpm-for/hard-techno). A search snippet claimed a 2024 median of 155 BPM rising from 143; this conflicts with the page's overall 146 median and was not verified.
- Attack Magazine's "Rolling Techno" beat: 131 BPM, 20% swing, 2-bar pattern. Kicks on beats 1 and 3 with ghost kicks before beats 2 and 4 for a "pendulum" effect. Low-passed toms alternate on every 16th for low-end roll. Claps are scattered across the grid with ghost velocities. 16th closed hats get an LFO on delay time. Open hats sit on beats 2 and 4, some pushed late at about 50% velocity. — [Attack Magazine: Rolling Techno](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/rolling-techno/)

**Trance**
- 125–150 BPM (another passage says 120–150), 16- or 32-beat phrases, kick on every downbeat, open hat on every upbeat. Builds are foreshadowed by long snare rolls that rise in velocity, frequency and volume. The supersaw comes from the Roland JP-8000. Tracks build to 1–2 peaks or drops. — [Wikipedia: Trance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music)
- Uplifting trance clusters around 138 BPM. Common elements are four-on-the-floor kicks, offbeat bass, rapid arpeggios, minor-key harmony, and long builds into beatless breakdowns that leave a melody or pad exposed before the climax. The JP-8000 Super Saw is seven detuned saws. — [Orphiq: What Is Trance Music?](https://orphiq.com/resources/what-is-trance-music)

**Drum & bass / jungle**
- DnB is usually 160–180 BPM. Since about 1996 most has sat at 170–180, and the mid-170s is the hallmark. The same elements at 140 BPM would be a "DnB-influenced breakbeat track". — [Wikipedia: Drum and bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_and_bass)
- Jungle (around 1994) drew on sound-system culture, MC chants, reggae/dancehall/ragga samples, dub basslines and "increasingly complex, heavily edited breakbeat percussion". — [Wikipedia: Drum and bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_and_bass)
- Neurofunk differs from techstep through cleaner production, intricate basslines, timbral change, and a shift to regular two-step rhythms away from jungle's dense polyrhythms. Later neuro uses wavetable and FM synthesis, filtering, distortion and resampling, with a consistent low component under a moving midrange. Reference artists: Noisia, Phace, Mefjus. — [Wikipedia: Neurofunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurofunk)
- Liquid DnB (from 1999, UK) uses similar basslines and bar layouts to other DnB but with fewer harsh sounds and more instrumentation, harmony, melody and ambience. Pioneers include High Contrast and Calibre. — [Wikipedia: Liquid drum and bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_funk); [Wikipedia: Drum and bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_and_bass)
- Early DnB was also called jungle (sped-up breakbeats). Later producers moved to programmed one-shots. Popular modern styles are jump-up (abrasive), neurofunk (technical) and liquid (soulful). — [Native Instruments Blog: How to produce a drum and bass track](https://blog.native-instruments.com/drum-and-bass/)

**Dubstep / riddim**
- Dubstep: sparse kick/snare/hat at a half-time feel (the example is an implied 71 BPM over 142 BPM), driven by sub-bass. Wobble bass uses an LFO on volume, distortion or filter cutoff. — [Wikipedia: Dubstep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubstep)
- Riddim: 140–150 BPM, built on a single tightly sculpted mid-range bass motif that loops and evolves by filtering and modulation. It has simple repetitive rhythms, percussive triplets, heavy use of silence and negative space, and minimal melody. Classic dubstep has more complex sound design and dynamic drops. — search summary of [BassGorilla: What is riddim dubstep](https://bassgorilla.com/what-is-riddim-dubstep/) and [DJ.Studio: BPM for dubstep](https://dj.studio/blog/bpm-for-dubstep) (BassGorilla page refused connection when fetched; snippet-level)

**Future bass**
- Future bass uses LFO- or automation-modulated filter and amplitude ("wobbly"), "twinkly" pitch-rising risers, arpeggiated chords, vocal chops and vocoders, in 4/4 at 130–175 BPM. — [Wikipedia: Future bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_bass)
- It is usually 140–160 BPM felt in half-time (snare on beat 3). Lush detuned supersaw chords bend and swell in pitch, and sidechain pumping is "a defining part of the genre's bounce". — [Violet Recording: How to Make Future Bass](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-make-future-bass/)

**UK garage / 2-step**
- 2-step: syncopated kicks that skip a beat, with shuffled or triplet percussion, off-beat snares, scattered rimshots and woodblocks, and syncopated basslines. — [Wikipedia: 2-step garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-step_garage)
- UKG has two kick options. Four-to-the-floor is more common in *speed garage* and adds drive. Two-step places kicks on syncopated or offbeat positions and is "the classic UK garage feel". The kick should be tight and punchy, not an EDM-style sub kick. — [The Producer School: How to Program UK Garage Drums](https://theproducerschool.com/blogs/featured-blogs/how-to-program-uk-garage-drums-complete-guide-for-producers)
- Future garage runs at 130–140 BPM with pitched vocal chops, warm filtered reese, dark atmospheres and vinyl crackle. — [Wikipedia: UK garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_garage)

**Synthwave, hyperpop, ambient, trailer**
- Synthwave draws on 1980s film, games and cartoons and composers like John Carpenter, Jean-Michel Jarre, Vangelis and Tangerine Dream. It is mostly instrumental, with common tempos of 80–118 BPM and upbeat tracks at 128–140. "Outrun" is a subgenre. — [Wikipedia: Synthwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthwave)
- Hyperpop is an exaggerated, self-referential take on pop: brash synth melodies, Auto-Tuned earworm vocals, excessive compression and distortion, and nostalgic 2000s internet references. 100 gecs are credited with popularising it. Glitchcore ("hyperpop on steroids") pushes pitch-shifters and rapid chopped vocals further. — [Wikipedia: Hyperpop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperpop)
- Ambient emphasises tone and atmosphere over structure or rhythm. It often lacks beat or structured melody and uses textural layers, sustained or repeated elements and nature soundscapes. Brian Eno coined the term in the mid-1970s (*Ambient 1: Music for Airports*, 1978). — [Wikipedia: Ambient music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_music)
- Trailer music is a subset of production music used for film previews. — [Wikipedia: Trailer music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailer_music)
- The trailer toolkit: braam (low brass or synth plus distortion and a long reverb tail), hit/impact (transient + body + sub layers), riser, whoosh, sub drop (falling sine under a hit), and a drone/tension bed. "The quiet before a hit matters as much as the hit itself." — [Violet Recording: Sound Design for Trailers](https://violetrecording.com/sound-design-for-trailers/)

**Key distinctions, condensed**
- Deep vs tech house: deep = slower (110–125), chord-rich, soulful vocals, hypnotic with no big builds. Tech = 124–128, chordless or one-chord, percussive, rolling mid-bass and swung 16ths. Both use 4/4 909 kicks.
- DnB vs jungle: DnB = programmed two-step skeleton (kick 1 and "and of 3", snares 2 and 4), clean mix, 170–176. Jungle = chopped, re-sequenced breakbeats (Amen etc.), dense polyrhythm, reggae/ragga samples, dub sub, often 160–170.
- Dubstep vs riddim: same 140 half-time skeleton. Dubstep varies bass sound design per phrase and uses darker sub-led space (classic) or growl variety (brostep). Riddim loops one simple mid-bass motif, often in triplets, with deliberate gaps.
- UKG vs 2-step: "UK garage" is the umbrella. 4x4 (speed) garage keeps a kick on every beat; 2-step removes kicks (typically beats 2 and 4, plus beat 1 of bar 2 in some grooves) so the swung snares and rims carry the groove.
- Trance vs techno vs tech house at similar BPM: trance is defined by melodic breakdowns, supersaw leads and arps; techno by rhythm and timbre with little harmony; tech house by swing and groove.

## Q2. What tempos and swing values are used, and how is the drum pattern constructed step by step?

### Takeaway
Tempo bands are fairly stable. House/tech house is 120–130, techno 125–140 with hard techno 145–160, trance 132–140, UKG 124–134, dubstep/riddim 140–150 half-time, DnB 172–176, and future bass 140–160 half-time. Swing is where genres split: techno/trance are almost straight, tech/deep house use moderate swing (55–65%), and garage uses heavy MPC-style swing (60–69%).

### Cited Findings
- **UK garage (Attack):** 124–132 BPM, **MPC16 swing 68/69**. Kick on the downbeat of each bar plus the offbeat of beat 3, with a double hit at the start of bar 2, pitched +3 st and short decay. Two rimshot samples act as a syncopated snare substitute at varying velocities (the second pitched +2 st). Soft closed hat (high-passed at 750 Hz). Cabasa on offbeats as an open-hat substitute. Crash at the start of a section. Drum bus: Ableton Drum Buss drive 25%, crunch 27%, 80% wet. Drum machines: MPC, Drumtrax, LinnDrum. — [Attack Magazine: UK Garage](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/uk-garage/)
- **Rolling 2-step (Attack):** 122–128 BPM, **60–65% swing**. Layered 909 and 808 kicks with *no kick on beat 1 of bar 2*. Snare on beats 2 and 4 (DM5 + pitched-up 808 layer). A hat on every offbeat with decay between closed and open. A live shaker on all offbeats with varied velocity and shortened hits. A tambourine playing 16ths with velocity variation, kept low in the mix. — [Attack Magazine: Rolling 2-Step](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/rolling-2-step-garage/)
- **Deep tech house (Attack):** 125–130 BPM, **60–65% swing**. Kick on quarters, open hat on offbeats, 16th shaker with velocity variation, clap on 2 and 4, low ghost snares, and processed hat percussion around the claps. — [Attack Magazine: Deep Tech House](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/deep-tech-house/)
- **Rolling techno (Attack):** 131 BPM, **20% swing**. Details are in Q1. — [Attack Magazine: Rolling Techno](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/rolling-techno/)
- **Trance:** kick on every downbeat and open hat on every upbeat, with snare rolls before transitions. — [Wikipedia: Trance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music)
- **DnB:** 174 BPM is "a good tempo to kick off". In the two-step beat, the second kick in the bar is pushed to the 8th note before the second snare, with snares on 2 and 4. — search summaries of [Computer Music via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/australia/computer-music/20200812/281878710716787) and [MusicRadar: 6 jungle and DnB grooves](https://www.musicradar.com/how-to/how-to-program-6-different-jungle-and-drum-n-bass-grooves) (snippet-level)
- **DnB:** sits at about 172–176 BPM. Get the 2-bar kick/snare skeleton grooving first, then layer a chopped Amen, Think or Apache break sliced at transients, with slices nudged off-grid. Add "a touch of swing so the off-beat hats lean late". Vary hat and ghost-snare velocity. Fills use 1/32 or 1/64 repeats with rising velocity. — [Violet Recording: How to Make Drum and Bass](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-make-drum-and-bass/)
- **Dubstep:** half-time with sparse kick, snare and hats (an implied 71 BPM feel at 142). — [Wikipedia: Dubstep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubstep)
- **Future bass:** half-time with the snare on beat 3. — [Violet Recording: How to Make Future Bass](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-make-future-bass/)
- **Hyperpop (NI tutorial):** tempo 205 BPM, 2-bar clip, all velocities 100. Snares on beat 3 of each bar. Kicks on beats 1 and 4 of bar 1 and on the final two 8th notes of bar 2. — [Native Instruments Blog: Hyperpop production 101](https://blog.native-instruments.com/hyperpop/). Other guides give 140–170+ BPM, or "135 or higher", with distorted/clipped kicks, metallic snares, glitches and pitched vocals. — [eMastered: How to Make Hyperpop](https://emastered.com/blog/how-to-make-hyperpop)
- **Synthwave:** steady 4/4 kick, gated-reverb snare on 2 and 4, crisp 16th hats, LinnDrum-style kits, 80–118 BPM. — search summary of [Output: best plugins for synthwave](https://output.com/blog/best-plugins-for-synthwave) and genre pages (snippet-level); tempo confirmed by [Wikipedia: Synthwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthwave)
- **Future garage:** 130–140 BPM. — [Wikipedia: UK garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_garage)
- **Hard techno:** 145–160 per [Futureproof](https://futureproofmusicschool.com/blog/what-is-hard-techno); measured median 146 per [Mixgraph](https://www.mixgraph.io/bpm-for/hard-techno).
- **Afro house:** BPM range 80–122, up to 160. — [Wikipedia: Afro house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro_house). Modern club afro house in practice clusters around 118–124 (researcher inference, not verified).

## Q3. What harmonic and melodic conventions are typical?

### Takeaway
Harmony runs from none (techno, riddim, hard techno), through modal minor vamps (house, afro house, trance, synthwave, trailer), to extended jazz/soul voicings (deep house, liquid DnB, UKG, future bass maj7/min7). Pop rests on I, IV, V and vi. The sound of each genre comes as much from voicing and timbre as from the progression: supersaws for trance and future bass, Rhodes and organ for deep house and garage, braams and low brass for trailers.

### Cited Findings
- Deep house uses "advanced chord structures", soft keyboard pads and soulful vocals. — [Wikipedia: Deep house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_house)
- Techno does not always follow Western harmonic practice. Rhythm and timbre design are the main parameters. — [Wikipedia: Techno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)
- Trance: minor-key harmony, rapid arpeggios, offbeat bass and a supersaw lead (seven detuned saws). — [Orphiq](https://orphiq.com/resources/what-is-trance-music). Arpeggios are a standard trance element. — [Wikipedia: Trance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music)
- Trance tutorial advice: write the breakdown first; keep harmony simple and resolve late with four minor-key chords (e.g. Am–F–C–G) and a suspended chord held "one bar longer than feels comfortable". A key lift (up a semitone or tone) in the final drop is a signature. The bass goes on offbeat 8ths between kicks. — search summary citing [Orphiq](https://orphiq.com/resources/what-is-trance-music) and trance tutorial pages (snippet-level; the key-lift claim was not seen on the fetched Orphiq text)
- Future bass: the major scale fits better than minor (it is happier than trap). Maj7 and min7 chords are used extensively, and triads are too thin unless the second note is doubled up an octave. The bass plays long notes while the chords play short rhythmic notes. Leads use short 16th/8th/quarter notes. Song parts are typically 4 bars. — [FeelYourSound: Future Bass tutorial](https://feelyoursound.com/articles/future-bass-tutorial/)
- Future bass voicings use 7ths, 9ths and add9 on a detuned supersaw or layered wavetable, with an envelope or LFO on pitch so chords "bend and swell into place". Slow filter or volume swells, voiced mid-register. A four-chord loop moving between a minor chord and its relative major gives the "uplifting-yet-wistful" mood. — [Violet Recording: How to Make Future Bass](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-make-future-bass/)
- Liquid DnB has more instrumentation, harmonies, melodies and ambience than other DnB, giving a "sentimental atmosphere". — [Wikipedia: Liquid drum and bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_funk)
- Hyperpop features brash synth melodies, Auto-Tuned earworm vocals and "saccharine chord progressions". — [Wikipedia: Hyperpop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperpop); [eMastered: How to Make Hyperpop](https://emastered.com/blog/how-to-make-hyperpop)
- Synthwave tutorial recipe: square-wave bass pulse, bright arpeggio, saw arp-bass with a tight filter envelope, slow-attack poly pad, square lead with slight portamento, and sidechain on pads and leads. — search summary of synthwave production pages (snippet-level, including [Output](https://output.com/blog/best-plugins-for-synthwave))
- Pop harmony: across 1,300 popular-song sections transposed to C, IV (F) and V (G) appear in *more* progressions than I (C). Omitting the tonic in sections is common. — [Hooktheory: I analyzed the chords of 1300 popular songs](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/i-analyzed-the-chords-of-1300-popular-songs-for-patterns-this-is-what-i-found/)
- 2-step uses syncopated basslines and pads or other instruments played percussively. — [Wikipedia: 2-step garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-step_garage)
- Future garage uses pitched vocal chops and warm filtered reese basses. — [Wikipedia: UK garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_garage)

### Inferences: harmony and lead cheat-sheet per genre (convention)
- **Classic house:** i–iv or i–VII vamps in minor/Dorian, or gospel I–IV–V–vi for vocal house. M1-style piano stabs on offbeats. Leads are diva vocals or filtered disco samples.
- **Deep house:** min9 → min7 → maj7 → dom7(sus) loops (e.g. Am9–Dm9–Gmaj7–Cmaj7, or ii–V voicings). Rhodes, organ stabs, warm pads. Melodic content is mostly a vocal or short keys motif.
- **Tech house:** often no chords. One-note or two-note stabs, vocal one-shots, plucked percussive synths. A minor key (A/F/G minor) sits well for kick and sub tuning.
- **Afro house:** modal minor (Dorian/Aeolian) 2-chord vamps, marimba/kalimba-like plucks, choir pads, chants, flute. Long evolving melodies over 8–16 bars.
- **Detroit techno:** string pads and minor 7th/sus chord stabs (e.g. "Strings of Life" piano and strings), sci-fi lead sequences.
- **Peak/hard techno:** atonal or single-note. 303 acid lines (16ths with accent and slide), trance-like stabs in peak-time.
- **Trance:** natural minor progressions such as i–VI–III–VII (Am–F–C–G) or vi–IV–I–V. Sus2/sus4 held before resolution. 16th gated or offbeat supersaw plucks and arps. Anthemic lead in the breakdown with a long-held top note. Optional +1/+2 semitone key lift for the last drop.
- **Liquid DnB:** maj7/min9/dom9 jazz voicings, ii–V–I, Rhodes, strings, soul vocals.
- **Neurofunk/dubstep/riddim:** minor or Phrygian, using b2 and tritone for menace. Melody lives in the bass's timbral movement. Short atmospheric pads in breakdowns.
- **Future bass:** major-leaning (IVmaj7–V–iii7–vi, the "royal road", fits well), sevenths everywhere. Chords play a rhythmic 16th pattern with pitch-bend swells. Lead is vocal chops plus a pitch-glide supersaw.
- **UKG/2-step:** min7/min9 and R&B extensions. Organ or "speed garage" bass hits. Chopped and pitched R&B vocal hooks.
- **Synthwave:** Aeolian i–VI–III–VII and i–VII–VI–VII. Arpeggiated 16th poly chords, saw/square leads with portamento, sax or guitar solos.
- **Modern pop:** I–V–vi–IV and rotations (vi–IV–I–V), I–IV–vi–V. Minor-key pop uses i–VI–III–VII. Topline repetition and short motifs; hook rhythm matters more than harmony.
- **Hyperpop:** bright major, sudden modulations and genre switches, pitched-up vocals, chiptune or "hypersaw" leads.
- **Ambient:** drones, maj7/add9/sus chords, Lydian colour, slow pads, loops of different lengths so the texture keeps shifting.
- **Trailer:** minor-key pedal-tone ostinati in the strings (16ths), i–VI–III–VII or i–VI–VII epic cadences, Phrygian b2 for threat. Brass and choir state the theme in Act 3. Braams sit on the tonic.

### Gaps
- No Hooktheory genre-specific (e.g. EDM vs pop) chord-frequency data was retrieved. Per-genre progressions above are convention.
- No source was retrieved for typical keys by genre (e.g. tech house's preference for F/G minor). Key choices are inference.

## Q4. How are tracks arranged, and how are builds and drops engineered?

### Takeaway
Club genres arrange in 8/16/32-bar blocks with DJ-friendly drum-led intros and outros (16–32 bars), and create contrast mainly by *subtracting* elements before adding FX. Pop uses verse/pre/chorus at about 3:00–3:15 with the first chorus around 30–45 s. Trailers follow a three-act escalation that ends on a "button".

### Cited Findings
- Trance uses 16- or 32-beat phrases and builds toward 1–2 peaks or drops, with snare-roll build-ups. — [Wikipedia: Trance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music)
- Trance arrangements commonly build over several minutes, then strip drums for a breakdown that exposes a melody or pad, then rebuild into a climax. — [Orphiq](https://orphiq.com/resources/what-is-trance-music)
- Deep house deliberately avoids the build-up/release emphasis of other dance genres. — [Wikipedia: Deep house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_house)
- Techno is DJ-friendly, mainly instrumental and made for continuous mixes. — [Wikipedia: Techno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)
- Mixed In Key guidance: 8- and 16-bar phrases are "a useful phrase shape, not a fixed rule". Map the strongest section first, then duplicate it into intro / main / contrast / return. Create contrast by subtraction (mute bass, remove percussion, shorten hooks) before adding effects. Lead into sections with a fill, held note, reversed sound or short gap, and avoid identical fills at every boundary. Club mixes need a clear intro and ending for DJs. Not every track needs a breakdown. — [Mixed In Key: How to arrange a dance music track](https://mixedinkey.com/captain-plugins/wiki/how-to-arrange-a-dance-music-track/)
- Generic EDM template: Intro 8/16/32, Verse 8/16/32, Chorus 8/16, Breakdown 8/16/32, Drop 8/16, Bridge 8/16/32, Outro 8/16/32. Intros are typically multiples of 16 bars and often add a new element every 32 beats. — search summary of [Mixed In Key](https://mixedinkey.com/?p=5367) and [SampleFocus: EDM song structure](https://samplefocus.com/blog/edm-song-structure-an-essential-guide/) (snippet-level)
- DnB arrangement: intro → drop 1 → breakdown → drop 2 → outro. The intro eases DJs in with drums and filtered bass. The drop brings break, sub and reese together. The breakdown strips to pads and vocals. Use risers, snare rolls and "a beat of silence right before each drop". Think in 8/16-bar blocks with something changing in each. — [Violet Recording: How to Make Drum and Bass](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-make-drum-and-bass/)
- Dubstep arrangement revolves around a tense build into an impactful bass "chorus" (the drop). Riddim uses silence and negative space to make drops hit, and evolves one bass loop instead of moving into new sections. — search summary of [DJ.Studio](https://dj.studio/blog/bpm-for-dubstep) and [BassGorilla](https://bassgorilla.com/what-is-riddim-dubstep/) (snippet-level)
- Future bass risers use "twinkly" gradual pitch rises over white-noise build-ups. — [Wikipedia: Future bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_bass)
- Trailer structure: Act 1 is quiet and emotional (piano, strings, mood). Act 2 builds intensity and adds rhythm and brass. Act 3 is the full climax (choir, percussion, impacts), ending with an optional button or stinger before the title card. Trailers are "built in escalating waves". — [Violet Recording: Sound Design for Trailers](https://violetrecording.com/sound-design-for-trailers/) plus search summary of trailer-music pages (snippet-level)
- Pop length: the 2025 Billboard Year-End Hot 100 median hit is **3:13** (mean 3:24, lifted by a few long hits). Length peaked at 4:39 in 1992 and bottomed at 3:13 in 2019. In 2019, 68% of hits sat in a 2:30–3:30 band. The share over 3:30 rose from 22% (2019) to 37% (2025). — [DEV Community: Are Songs Really Getting Shorter? 6,618 Hot 100 hits](https://dev.to/tonywangca/are-songs-really-getting-shorter-6618-hot-100-hits-1959-2025-4cdg)
- The average Spotify-charting song was about 3:00 in 2024, roughly 15 s shorter than 2023 and 30 s shorter than 2019. Pop, hip-hop, alternative, dance and Latin all got shorter. Nearly 20% of 2024 Grammy songs were under 3:00, and "Espresso" and "360" were short Record of the Year nominees. — [Hypebot: Songs are getting shorter](https://www.hypebot.com/hypebot/2025/04/songs-are-getting-shorter-and-the-surprising-truth-behind-it.html) (via search summary). The "~3:00" (Spotify-charting) and "3:13 median" (Hot 100) figures measure different populations.
- First chorus: one analysis of Hot 100 #1s from 2020–2023 reported the first chorus arriving at an average of about 38 s, versus 55+ s in earlier decades. Pop intros/outros are usually 4–8 bars, and the hook recurs every 30–45 s. — search summary of hit-song analysis pages (e.g. [Hit Songs Deconstructed](https://accelerator.hitsongsdeconstructed.com/?p=3268), [Simon Napier-Bell Substack](https://simonnapierbell.substack.com/p/no-more-foreplay)). Snippet-level; the exact originating source was not verified. The same snippet's claim of a "2:40 global average" conflicts with the Hot 100 3:13 median.

### Inferences: arrangement templates (bar counts; convention unless cited above)

**House / tech house / afro house (DJ / extended mix, 124–126 BPM, about 6–7 min)**
| Section | Bars | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Intro A | 16 | Kick + hats + perc only (DJ mix-in) |
| Intro B | 16 | + clap, + bass (filtered open), + first hook tease |
| Groove 1 | 32 | Full drums + bass + hook; change something every 8 |
| Breakdown | 16 | Drop kick and bass; chords, vocal and FX; filter sweep |
| Build | 8 | Snare/clap roll, riser, high-pass on the master group, 1-beat gap |
| Groove 2 / drop | 32 | Full energy + new layer (vocal, extra perc) |
| Break 2 (optional) | 8–16 | Short tease |
| Groove 3 | 16–32 | Variations |
| Outro | 32 | Remove musical parts in reverse; drums-only last 16 |
Deep house: same blocks with shallower breakdowns and no big risers (cited: avoids build/release).

**Techno (Detroit / peak / hard, 6–8 min):** Intro 32 (kick + hats), main A 32, tension section 16–32 (remove kick, add rumble or acid automation), main B 32–64, outro 32. Changes every 8 or 16 bars are mostly filter, FX send or a percussion layer. Peak-time adds an 8–16 bar break with a riser and a trance-like stab.

**Trance (138 BPM, extended about 6–8 min; radio edit about 3:00–3:30)**
| Section | Bars |
|---|---|
| Intro (DJ) | 32 |
| Build / main groove (bass + arps) | 32 |
| Breakdown 1 (beatless, pads + lead theme) | 16–32 |
| Build (snare roll 8–16 bars, riser, filter up) | 8–16 |
| Climax 1 | 32 |
| Mid groove / second breakdown | 16–32 |
| Climax 2 (optional key lift) | 32 |
| Outro (DJ) | 32 |

**DnB (174 BPM, about 4.5–6 min)**: Intro 32 (drums + filtered bass, DJ), Build 8–16, Drop 1 32 (switch-up at bar 16), Breakdown 16–32, Build 8 (snare roll + one beat of silence), Drop 2 32 (new bass variation, "VIP" switch), Outro 32. At 174 BPM, 32 bars ≈ 44 s.

**Dubstep / riddim (140–150 BPM, about 3.5–4.5 min)**: Intro 16–32, Build 8 (riser + snare roll + vocal), Drop 1 16–32 (call-and-response bass in 2/4-bar phrases), Breakdown 16, Build 8, Drop 2 16–32 (new bass sounds; riddim = same motif with switch-ups), Outro 16.

**Future bass (150 BPM half-time, about 3–4 min, pop form)**: Intro 8, Verse 16, Build 8 (twinkly riser, pitch-up vocal chop, kick removed), Drop 16 (pitch-bent supersaw chords, chops), Verse 2 8–16, Build 8, Drop 2 16 (variation), Bridge/outro 8.

**UK garage / 2-step (130 BPM, 4–6 min club, about 3:30 vocal)**: Intro 16 (drums), Verse 16, Chorus/hook 16 (vocal chop), Break 8, Verse 16, Chorus 16, Dub section 16 (bass-led), Outro 16.

**Synthwave (100 BPM, 3.5–5 min)**: Intro 8–16 (pad + arp), Verse 16, Chorus 16, Verse 16, Chorus 16, Solo 16 (sax/guitar/lead), Final chorus 16, Outro 8–16 (fade or ring-out).

**Modern pop (target 2:45–3:15; first chorus by about 0:30–0:45)**: Intro 4 (or cold open on the hook), Verse 1 8, Pre-chorus 4–8, Chorus 8, Post-chorus 4–8 (instrumental or vocal-chop hook), Verse 2 8 (shorter or busier), Pre 4, Chorus 8, Post 4–8, Bridge 8 (strip-down), Final chorus 8–16 (lift: add harmonies, key change optional), Outro 0–4.

**Hyperpop (2:00–2:45)**: short, maximal sections with abrupt genre or tempo switch-ups, beat drops into silence, glitch stutters at transitions, and often a final "breakdown" that halves tempo or explodes into distortion.

**Ambient/chill**: through-composed. Layers enter one at a time and change slowly over minutes; tracks run 5–20 min. Chill/downtempo uses loose 16–32 bar sections.

**Cinematic trailer (2:00–2:30)**: Act 1 (0:00–0:40) is a sparse piano/strings motif, drone bed and an occasional hit. Act 2 (0:40–1:30) brings in ostinato strings, pulses, percussion and brass swells, with energy stepping up every 8 bars. A riser and 1–2 beats of silence lead into Act 3 (1:30–2:15): full choir, brass, taiko and braams on downbeats. Button/stinger (last 3–10 s): a final hit plus a sub drop after silence.

**Build/drop toolkit (cross-genre)**
- *Subtract first:* remove kick/bass 4–16 bars before the drop.
- *Rhythmic acceleration:* snare/clap roll 4ths → 8ths → 16ths → 32nds with a velocity ramp (cited for trance and DnB).
- *Spectral rise:* high-pass the music bus, white-noise riser, pitch-rising "twinkle" riser (future bass), uplifter.
- *Gap:* half a beat to 1 beat of silence before the drop (cited for DnB; standard elsewhere), or a vocal "drop" tag.
- *Impact:* crash plus a sub-drop/impact on the downbeat. The kick returns on beat 1.
- *Variation:* the second drop changes bass patch, adds a layer or key-lifts (trance), and avoids identical fills at every boundary (cited, Mixed In Key).
